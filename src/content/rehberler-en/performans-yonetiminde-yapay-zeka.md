Performance management is one of the HR processes that managers spend the most time on and employees worry about most. Generative AI can speed up some steps of this process: drafting feedback, tracking progress against goals, and summarizing scattered data. However, because the decisions affect people's careers, errors, bias and transparency problems carry heavier consequences than in other areas. This guide explains where you can use the tool safely, where you should stop, and how to protect employees' rights. For the broader framework, you can also read the guide on [AI in human resources](/rehber/insan-kaynaklarinda-yapay-zeka/).

## Where AI helps in performance management

The most concrete benefit is in writing. Instead of writing evaluation texts from scratch, managers can start from a draft. For example, PeopleGoal's roundup in [its compilation](https://www.peoplegoal.com/blog/ai-in-performance-management/) reports that JPMorgan Chase allowed managers to use an internal AI chatbot to prepare performance review write-ups, and that the bank's own guidance described this as a “starting point” that does not replace human judgment. This is a secondary source; the bank's own guidance is not accessible from here. Still, it captures the right logic: the tool produces a draft, and judgment stays with the human.

The second benefit is on the data side. Gathering scattered sources such as goal updates, project notes and meeting records into a single summary may reduce the tendency of managers to remember only the last few weeks at year-end. The limit is clear, though: the tool only sees the data it is given and cannot correct missing or wrong input.

## How to prepare feedback and evaluation drafts

What you give the tool when drafting determines the result. A good starting point could be:

- Provide only measurable and documented observations: completed work, delivery dates, feedback received.
- Ask the tool for example-based sentences, not adjectives that judge behavior.
- Strip out details that imply the employee's name, gender or age, and remember that the model does not need this information.
- Read the output line by line and compare what is written with your own notes to confirm it actually happened. Models can describe a success or failure that never occurred in fluent language (see [hallucination](/sozluk/halusinasyon/)).

Betterworks' blog treats [automation bias](https://www.betterworks.com/magazine/ai-performance-management), the tendency of managers to accept an AI-suggested summary or feedback without question, as a risk. Since the source is a software company, it is not independent research, but it is a practical warning: because accepting a draft is easy, make questioning it a deliberate step. The guide on [how to write good prompts](/rehber/iyi-istem-nasil-yazilir/) is also useful for the writing side.

## Goal tracking and performance data analysis

In goal tracking, AI's role usually involves reminders, progress summaries and making at-risk goals visible. The point to watch here is not confusing what is measurable with what is valuable. Lines of code written, emails answered or meeting attendance are easy to measure, but they may not reflect the quality of work or contribution to the team.

Using data gathered from monitoring employees in evaluations is a separate risk area; see the guide on [employee monitoring and AI](/rehber/calisan-izleme-ve-yapay-zeka/). As a general rule, employees should know in advance which data will be used in evaluations. Assessing people against criteria that were not announced beforehand weakens both trust and defensibility.

## Bias risk: AI and humans alike

According to a law firm's article, a model trained on historical data may [learn the same pattern](https://amberboydlaw.com/ai-performance-reviews-are-they-discriminatory-under-indiana-law/) in an organization where certain groups received low scores in the past. This is a classic form of [AI bias](/sozluk/yapay-zeka-onyargisi/): the model appears neutral but carries forward old inequalities.

On the other hand, human evaluation is not flawless either. In a 2025 field study reported by Fast Company, managers at a US-based multinational financial services firm [anchored to prior-year ratings](https://www.fastcompany.com/91642369/3-places-performance-review-bias-hides) when they could not see employees' self-assessments. This did not change gender or racial differences; the change was seen only among newer employees with no self-assessment history. So the problem is not only “algorithm or human,” but the design of the process.

Employees' perceptions on this are also interesting. In a scenario experiment with 120 participants by Yunshil Cha, employees preferred AI evaluations when they expected negative bias and human evaluations when they expected positive bias. A negative and biased human evaluation increased the thought of quitting more than an AI evaluation in the same situation; details are in [the summary from the University of New Hampshire](https://www.unh.edu/unhtoday/2025/02/ai-vs-human-could-algorithms-be-key-fairer-employee-evaluations). The study relies on fictional scenarios, not the outcome of a real workplace, but it shows that trust depends on the source.

## Transparency and the legal framework

According to the EU's official AI Act service desk, if a system evaluates employees' performance or allocates tasks based on it, it falls under point 4(b) of Annex III. The same page notes that classification may change if the evaluation only supports a managerial task, and that classification is made case by case ([AI Act service desk](https://ai-act-service-desk.ec.europa.eu/en/employment-0)). Because sources give different information about the timeline for obligations, check the current official text before implementation. If you have ties to the EU, the guide on [the EU AI Act and Türkiye](/rehber/ab-yapay-zeka-yasasi-ve-turkiye/) is a good starting point.

There is no AI regulation in Türkiye specific to performance evaluation; current interpretations rely on the opinions of law firms. One law firm writes that basing important decisions such as performance or hiring solely on AI output is [problematic](https://www.cetinavukatlik.com/is-yerinde-yapay-zeka-kullanimi-hukuki-riskler/), and that an AI evaluation should not be the single and final basis. According to another consultancy article, the relevant article of the KVKK (Turkey's Personal Data Protection Law) grants a [right to object](https://sheltron.com.tr/blog/yapay-zeka-kvkk-uyumu-kontrol-listesi/) only when a result adverse to the person arises solely from automated analysis. Confirm the scope of these interpretations with a legal expert; this guide is not legal advice. For details, see the guide on [KVKK and AI tools](/rehber/kvkk-ve-yapay-zeka-araclari/).

## How to put employees' right to appeal into practice

Gowindmill's practical policy template recommends that employees be informed about AI use, be able to request the sources the evaluation is based on, correct inaccurate information, and appeal the outcome. The template also distinguishes a correction (a factual error) from an appeal (an interpretation or evaluation difference), and recommends that the appeal be reviewed by [a person who did not make the original decision](https://gowindmill.com/resources/guides/ai-performance-review-policy/). This is a software firm's template, not binding text, but it offers a workable skeleton.

You can make this concrete within the company as follows:

1. Notify employees in writing about the step at which AI is used in the evaluation.
2. Give employees the ability to see the data that enters the evaluation and correct factual errors.
3. Set a clear channel and deadline for appeals, and assign the review to a person who did not make the decision.
4. Record the outcome of every appeal and use recurring errors for process improvement.

Adding these steps to a [company AI policy](/rehber/sirket-ici-yapay-zeka-politikasi/) gathers the rules in one place.

## Company examples and products

Be cautious about company examples: most of the examples we could see come from third-party blogs and could not be verified through the companies' own statements. The JPMorgan example is described above. On the product side, performance management platforms such as Lattice, Leapsome, 15Five and Culture Amp are reported to offer AI features; however, such comparison articles and vendors' own satisfaction figures do not count as independent evidence. We could not find a reliable example of a company in Türkiye using AI in performance management. When choosing a tool, look at your own pilot results and where the data is processed rather than the vendor's figures.

## How to get started

Begin with a small, reversible pilot. First use it only for drafting write-ups, and do not hand scoring or promotion recommendations to the tool. During the pilot, track how much managers change the drafts; if they never change them, that is a sign of automation bias. Compare score distributions across groups regularly, and if you see unexpected gaps, pause the process and investigate the cause.

Preparation of employees matters too: to learn how managers and employees use the tool, see the guide on [AI training for employees](/rehber/calisanlara-yapay-zeka-egitimi/). Finally, AI does not make performance management fair; what makes it fair is a transparent process, human oversight and a working appeal path. The tool can help within that structure, but it cannot replace it.
