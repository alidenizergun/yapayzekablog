AI tools have become part of everyday work at law firms. But legal practice is a profession where the cost of an error falls on the client and the lawyer. This guide explains which tasks AI can help with, the difference between legal-specific tools and general assistants, and what to check on confidentiality and professional liability. The text is not legal advice. For decisions on AI use in your own firm, consult professional rules and the relevant legislation.

## Where it is used

The most commonly cited uses of AI in law firms are:

- **Legal research:** Sketching an initial framework on a topic, identifying terms to search for and possible sources.
- **Petition and contract drafting:** Getting a first draft quickly instead of starting from a blank page, or simplifying and reorganizing existing text.
- **Document summarization and Q&A:** Pulling relevant sections from a long file, or asking a specific question and seeking answers grounded in the document.
- **Client communication:** Simplifying the language of client information notes and drafting emails.

In all of these tasks, AI produces the first draft or first read, and the final check remains with you. Because the success of tools in areas such as client communication and contract drafting varies by firm and practice area, test them on your own samples before wider use. For the contract side, you can also see the [AI in contract review](/rehber/sozlesme-incelemesinde-yapay-zeka/) guide.

## What adoption figures say

According to [8am's 2026 legal industry report](https://www.8am.com/reports/generative-ai-legal-guide/), 69% of legal professionals use AI at work. In 2025 the figure was 31%. The same source says use of general-purpose tools such as ChatGPT and Claude has doubled.

Transparency is less clear. In a survey of more than 250 legal professionals by [Everlaw](https://www.everlaw.com/2026-legal-ai-adoption-and-impact-report/), 31% of respondents disclose generative AI use across all their legal work. Another 26% do not disclose it because they consider AI an internal tool. For obligations such as disclosure to clients or courts, check the relevant professional rules and institutional guidance separately.

## Legal-specific tools: Harvey and CoCounsel

Harvey and CoCounsel are the two most compared products for law firms. CoCounsel's distinguishing feature is its Westlaw connection. [According to one comparison](https://www.layer3labs.io/comparisons/harvey-vs-cocounsel), the product is built on Westlaw case law and Practical Law guides, which are Thomson Reuters' legal content. Grounding answers in specific content aims to reduce the risk of fabricated information compared with a general chatbot, but it does not eliminate that risk.

Comparative data for document tasks is limited. According to results from the Vals Legal AI Report's February 27, 2025 update, [reported on a secondary blog](https://www.aivortex.io/legal/compare/harvey-ai-vs-cocounsel/), Harvey Assistant scored 94.8% on document Q&A and CoCounsel scored 89.6%. On document summarization, CoCounsel scored 77.2% and Harvey scored 72.1%. These figures are from 2025 and tied to a specific test setup, so they do not amount to a direct comparison of today's versions.

The practical distinction in the sources is that research-focused teams lean toward CoCounsel, while teams with heavy drafting and workflow needs lean toward Harvey. This is an opinion. Sources also conflict on pricing. [One pricing comparison site](https://thelegalprompts.com/blog/ai-legal-tools-pricing-comparison) notes that Harvey does not publish its prices. So request a current, written quote from the vendor and ask for a trial period.

## When general assistants are enough

General assistants such as ChatGPT or Claude can be useful for work that does not involve client information: simplifying a text, drafting an email, having a concept explained, or having your own text reviewed. For good results, the habit of [writing good prompts](/rehber/iyi-istem-nasil-yazilir/) also matters.

The limits of general tools are that they are not tied to legal sources, and that where your data goes when you upload a file depends on the terms of use. Legal-specific tools try to close these two gaps with source links and enterprise security agreements. Even so, do not decide which tool is safe without reading the contract terms.

## Confidentiality and personal data

Client files are covered by professional secrecy and often contain personal data. A [law firm article](https://sinanogluhukuk.com/tr/makaleler/kvkk-avukatlar-rehberi-yapay-zeka-yurt-disi-aktarim) examining KVKK's implementation guidance for lawyers argues that uploading a file to an AI tool may count as a cross-border transfer. Before uploading files to a service whose servers are abroad, make sure to complete this assessment.

Practical steps:

1. Where possible, work by removing or changing information such as names, Turkish ID numbers and addresses.
2. Check whether the tool uses your data for model training, using its contract and settings.
3. Find out which country the data is processed in.
4. Put in writing, within the firm, which types of information must never be uploaded under any circumstances.

For general principles, see the guide to [protecting personal data when using AI](/rehber/yapay-zeka-kullanirken-kisisel-verileri-koruma/).

## Professional rules and liability

The Union of Turkish Bar Associations (TBB) published the [Guidance on the Use of AI in Legal Practice](https://www.barobirlik.org.tr/Haberler/tbb-avukatlikta-yapay-zeka-kullanimina-iliskin-tavsiye-rehberini-yayimladi-86648), based on the Artificial Intelligence and Legal Practice Workshop held in Ankara on February 14-15, 2026. [A news outlet reporting on the guidance](https://haberkesir.com.tr/2026/08/28/tbbden-avukatlar-icin-yapay-zeka-kullanim-rehberi-mesleki-sorumluluk-yapay-zekaya-devredilemez/) summarizes its core message in its headline: professional responsibility cannot be delegated to AI. In practice this is simple. You sign every text the tool produces, and you are responsible for its accuracy.

The best-known risk is fabricated information, known as hallucination. A model can produce a decision or article number that does not exist and phrase it confidently. Therefore:

- Open and read every cited decision and statutory article in the primary source.
- Compare summarized documents with the original text on key points.
- Check dates, amounts and names one by one.

For details, see the guide on the [risk of fabricated case law](/rehber/hukukta-yapay-zeka-uydurma-ictihat-riski/). For a general overview of the situation in Turkey, see the [AI in law in Türkiye](/rehber/turkiyede-hukukta-yapay-zeka/) page.

## How to get started

Start with small, low-risk tasks, such as simplifying an email you wrote yourself or summarizing a public document. Then follow this order:

1. **Set rules:** Define in a written [internal AI policy](/rehber/sirket-ici-yapay-zeka-politikasi/) which tools can be used and with what data.
2. **Test:** Compare several tools on the same task, using anonymized examples from your real practice areas.
3. **Set up a verification routine:** Decide who will check each output and against which source. Human review of every output is essential.
4. **Consider informing clients:** For the scope of disclosure obligations, check your bar association's guidance and professional rules.

AI is a helper that saves time in legal work. It does not replace legal judgment or responsibility. For a specific case, consult your bar association or a colleague experienced in the field.
