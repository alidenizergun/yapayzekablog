AI in healthcare in Turkey appears in three separate places: public research and domestic production goals, digital services citizens use directly, such as e-Nabız, and local startups attracting investment. What they share is that the data they process is health data. This guide summarizes which initiatives can be verified with sources, where details are missing, and what to watch for when working with health data. For the bigger picture, see the [Healthcare AI: What's Happening Today](/rehber/saglikta-yapay-zeka-bugun-neler-yapiliyor/) guide.

## Public sector efforts

The Türkiye Health Institutes Presidency (TÜSEB), which operates under the Ministry of Health, lists the development of AI-based health technologies as a goal in its [2026 performance program](https://files.tuseb.gov.tr/tuseb/files/dokumanlar/2026-01-28-10-43-56.pdf). The activity is named “Health Data Research and Artificial Intelligence Applications Research in Health.” The same document also sets a goal of domestic production of medical devices, diagnostic kits and AI products.

The Türkiye Health Data Research and Artificial Intelligence Applications Institute (TÜYZE), which is affiliated with TÜSEB, organizes scientific events. The institute's [workshops page](https://tuyze.tuseb.gov.tr/bilimsel-etkinlikler/calistaylar) lists a workshop announced for February 2, 2026, titled “Data Infrastructure Development Workshop for AI in Healthcare.” That data infrastructure has its own workshop suggests the core obstacle for AI in healthcare is data, not models.

Health Minister Kemal Memişoğlu also highlighted domestic production and the integration of AI in healthcare on October 16, 2026. According to a [BHA report](https://www.alparslandiyari.com/haber/saglik-bakani-yerli-uretim-ve-yapay-zekanin-onemine-vurgu-yapti_77798/), he spoke about integrating AI into the health sector and about the contribution of value-based reimbursement models to health tourism. These are directional statements; they include no timeline for when a specific product will be used at which hospital.

## e-Nabız and the RİTMİK assistant

e-Nabız is the public platform where citizens access their own health records. Its AI-powered assistant, RİTMİK, was introduced by Deputy Health Minister Assoc. Prof. Şuayıp Birinci at the 12th Turkish Medical World Congress. According to [press reports](https://gulcehaber.com/gundem/yapay-zeka-destekli-e-nabiz-asistani-ritmik-tanitildi-423786.html), RİTMİK works with natural-language commands, and users will be able to access their test results, prescriptions and health history, and handle appointments.

The assistant is reported to be built on e-Nabız's data infrastructure. However, the coverage contains no details on how data is processed, KVKK compliance or access security. Nothing definitive can be said on these points, so the best approach is to follow official statements and e-Nabız's privacy notices.

In practical terms, keep this in mind: an assistant that takes natural-language commands may make it easier to reach your records, but it does not interpret what a test result means medically, and it should not. Consult your doctor for interpretation.

## Why health data is protected separately

Health data falls into the special category of personal data under Law No. 6698 on the Protection of Personal Data (KVKK). This reflects the high risk of harm to individuals if such data is leaked or misused. For details, the [What Is Health Data? Processing Conditions Under KVKK](https://dijilegal.com/kavram/saglik-verisi) page is a good starting point; processing conditions are set out in Article 6 of the law.

A summary of Personal Data Protection Board decision No. 2021/761, available on the [KVKK website](https://www.kvkk.gov.tr/Icerik/7137/2021-761), addresses health data shared by hospital staff without authorization with a parent who did not have custody. The decision is not directly about AI, but it shows that even inside a hospital, who can access data is scrutinized. Because AI systems can reach large numbers of records, the same care applies to them.

If you are planning an AI project, answer these questions first:

- Which data will be processed, and is it truly necessary?
- What is the legal basis for processing the data?
- Who will be able to access the data, and will that access be logged?
- Will the data go to a third-party service (for example, a cloud-based model)?

For the legal side, consult an expert in health law and KVKK.

## Hospitals and local startups

For hospital-level AI applications (image analysis, triage and routing, for example), the most reliable source is the official announcement from the institution or hospital. Distinguish pilot projects from systems in continuous use. The phrase “AI-powered” in a news story does not mean the system is used in clinical decision-making. For examples in imaging, see the [AI in medical imaging](/rehber/tibbi-goruntulemede-yapay-zeka/) guide.

On the startup side, news that Viseur AI received investment appeared on [Webrazzi](https://webrazzi.com/2026/05/20/yerli-saglik-girisimi-viseur-ai-350-bin-dolar-yatirim-aldi/), and the headline gives the amount as $350,000. Investment news can show that a company is growing, but it does not prove a product's clinical accuracy or widespread use in healthcare facilities. When evaluating a local solution, ask for independent validation studies, regulatory approvals and data processing terms.

## Regulation: pending bills

Turkey is debating a comprehensive framework specific to AI. [NPartners' comparison](https://npartners.com.tr/turkiyede-2026-yapay-zeka-kanunu-tbmmye-sunulan-uc-kanun-teklifinin-detayli-karsilastirilmasi/) covers three bills submitted to the Turkish Grand National Assembly (TBMM). Bills are not binding until enacted, so do not describe a bill's provisions as current rules without checking its status. The [EU AI Act and Turkey](/rehber/ab-yapay-zeka-yasasi-ve-turkiye/) guide, which looks at the EU's approach from the perspective of companies in Turkey, may also interest you.

What is actually applied now is KVKK, alongside the legislation governing health services. In most cases, then, the first compliance test for a healthcare AI project happens on the data protection side.

## Practical tips for individuals

- Use official channels (such as e-Nabız) to access your own health records. Assistant interfaces may be convenient, but the security of your account is your responsibility.
- Do not enter your name, Turkish ID number or test reports into general-purpose chatbots. For details, see the [protecting personal data when using AI](/rehber/yapay-zeka-kullanirken-kisisel-verileri-koruma/) guide.
- If you want general information from an AI, treat its answers as a list of questions to bring to your doctor, not as a diagnosis. The [using AI for health questions](/rehber/saglik-sorularinda-yapay-zeka-kullanimi/) guide offers direction.
- Remember that AI responses can contain made-up information (hallucinations).

## Starting steps for institutions and startups

- Start with a small, well-defined use case, such as appointment routing, which touches patient data the least.
- Where possible, work with anonymized data, and document which fields are truly necessary.
- Require a health professional to review the system's outputs.
- Get written answers from your vendor on where data is stored and whether it is used to train its models.
- Determine the legal basis and the notice process with a KVKK specialist.

## Limits and risks

AI can reduce workload in healthcare, but it cannot replace physicians. Models can give wrong or fabricated answers, and their performance may drop for patients who differ from the groups they were trained on. Launch announcements in the news do not show how a system performs in real life. When deciding on a product, weigh official sources, independent assessments and data security terms together. This guide is for general information only; consult a qualified professional for medical and legal matters.
