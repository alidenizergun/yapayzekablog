HR teams have started experimenting with AI across many tasks, from writing job postings and screening candidates to creating training content and answering employee questions. But the same tools also touch decisions that directly affect candidates' and employees' lives. This guide summarizes what the research shows, which tasks can reasonably be expected to benefit from AI, where caution is needed, and how to get started.

## Where adoption stands

According to a summary by [Pin](https://www.pin.com/blog/ai-adoption-recruiting-report/) of SHRM's 2026 report, 39% of HR teams use AI in talent functions, and 46% expect to use it by year-end. These figures come from secondary summaries, and different pages cite different sample sizes, so read them as a general trend rather than precise rates.

Recruiting leads among the use cases. According to a [Noon compilation](https://www.noon.ai/blog/articles/355-how-many-companies-use-ai-in-hiring), recruiting is the most common area at 27%, followed by HR technology (21%), learning and development (17%) and employee experience (14%). In other words, outside recruiting, adoption is still at an early stage, which leaves more room for experimentation.

Momentum is strong among recruiters. According to LinkedIn's January 2026 research, [as reported by CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/make-it/2026/01/11/ai-dominate-hiring-2026-linkedin-execs-top-tips-stand-out.html), 93% of recruiters plan to increase their use of AI in 2026.

## Job postings and candidate screening

In a [blog compilation](https://copilot.recruitaisuite.com/blog/ai-recruiting-statistics-2026/) based on SHRM's 2025 Talent Trends survey, the most common recruiting uses are writing job postings (66%), screening applications (44%), automating candidate sourcing (32%) and communicating with candidates (29%). This figure comes from the compilation, not directly from SHRM's own page.

Writing job postings is one of the lowest-risk starting points. You can give AI the duties and requirements of a position, ask for a draft, and then refine it with your team. Before publishing, check the output for unnecessary requirements, discriminatory or exclusionary language, and promises your company does not actually offer. For help with prompt writing, see the guide on [how to write a good prompt](/rehber/iyi-istem-nasil-yazilir/).

Candidate screening is more sensitive. Having AI summarize applications or suggest a shortlist can save time, but relying on a system whose ranking criteria you do not know can scale bias. We cover this topic in detail in our guide on AI in hiring and bias.

## Interview scheduling and pre-screening

According to LinkedIn research, 66% of recruiters plan to increase their use of AI for pre-screening interviews, and about 70% of them believe it will allow for more valuable conversations with candidates ([HR Dive](https://www.hrdive.com/news/recruiters-increasing-their-ai-usage-as-pressure-to-hire-intensifies/809051/)).

In practice, you can think of AI as a helper for the following tasks:

- Preparing a structured interview question set for each position
- Drafting interview schedules and information emails for candidates
- Summarizing interview notes in a shared format

The key principle here is consistency: apply the same questions and the same evaluation framework to all candidates. If you use automated pre-screening, make sure a human reviews the decision so that no candidate is eliminated solely on the basis of an AI output.

## Training content, employee assistants, performance and feedback

**Training content.** In Kariyer.net's research, training and development stands out as the area with the largest increase in AI use ([Anadolu Agency](https://www.aa.com.tr/tr/isdunyasi/genel/kariyernet-yapay-zeka-ve-insan-kaynaklari-arastirmasi-2026nin-sonuclarini-paylasti/705207)). Training plan drafts, presentation outlines, knowledge check questions and sample case texts can be prepared quickly with AI. A subject-matter expert should verify the accuracy of the content, since generative AI can produce made-up information. A guide on AI training for employees can also help your staff learn to use AI themselves.

**Assistants for employee questions.** A chat assistant for frequently asked questions about leave, benefits and process steps is an appealing idea. To make it work, ensure the assistant answers only based on current company policies, shows the document it relies on, and refers the question to an HR specialist when it is unsure. For sensitive topics such as discipline, health or salary, prefer routing to a human over automated answers. Pay attention to which tool employee personal data is entered into; for details, see the guide on [protecting personal data when using AI](/rehber/yapay-zeka-kullanirken-kisisel-verileri-koruma/).

**Performance and feedback.** Supportive uses are reasonable, such as helping managers write clearer and more constructive feedback or summarizing meeting notes. Systems that determine outcomes such as scoring, promotion or dismissal, however, require far greater care. The regulation described below also classifies systems used for employee evaluation as high-risk.

## Findings from Türkiye

According to Kariyer.net's 'AI and Human Resources Research 2026', which included 318 employer representatives and HR professionals, the use of AI in companies' HR processes rose by 9 points compared with last year. The most common area for AI is recruiting and candidate screening ([Anadolu Agency](https://www.aa.com.tr/tr/isdunyasi/genel/kariyernet-yapay-zeka-ve-insan-kaynaklari-arastirmasi-2026nin-sonuclarini-paylasti/705207)).

In the same survey, 61% of respondents think AI should take on a major share of responsibility for application screening and shortlisting, 60% for data analysis and reporting, and 56% for drafting job postings. However, 90% say that final hiring decisions should rest mainly with humans. This picture shows a clear preference: some tasks can be delegated to AI, but the final decision should remain with people.

## Risks and regulation

The most important risk is bias: if a system learns from past hiring data, it can repeat the patterns in that data. The second risk is transparency; if candidates and employees do not know how decisions are made, trust erodes. The third is personal data: CVs and employee information are sensitive.

The EU's AI Act places hiring and employee evaluation systems in the high-risk category. According to a [compliance page](https://www.regulation-ai.eu/en/sectors/hr-recruitment/), this covers CV screening, candidate ranking and targeted job advertising. Sources are not consistent about when obligations for high-risk systems begin; [Payoneer's page](https://www.payoneer.com/resources/workforce-management/hr-and-compliance/eu-ai-act-recruitment/) gives December 2, 2027. For dates and scope, rely on the official text and your legal adviser. For the broader framework, see the guide on [the EU AI Act and Türkiye](/rehber/ab-yapay-zeka-yasasi-ve-turkiye/).

In Türkiye, processing candidate and employee data with AI tools requires legal assessment under Turkey's Personal Data Protection Law (KVKK); consult a lawyer on this matter.

## How to get started

1. **Separate the processes.** List which tasks, such as drafting text, are low-risk and which ones, such as those that affect decisions about candidates or employees, are high-risk.
2. **Start with a small pilot.** Choose a task whose output you can easily check, such as a job posting draft, an interview question set or training content.
3. **Define human review.** Put in writing who will approve each output and against which criteria.
4. **Limit data.** Do not enter identity details and sensitive information into tools unless necessary.
5. **Set rules.** Clarify what employees may do with AI and which tools they may use through an internal AI policy.
6. **Measure and collect feedback.** Regularly review time savings, error types and feedback from candidates and employees.

While sources citing SHRM data report productivity and work quality gains for users, these figures rely on secondary sources. Verify the benefits in your own process using your own measurements.
