For a business entering e-export, the first barrier is often language: hundreds of product pages, category names and product features must be carried into the target market's language. AI speeds up this work, but translation and localization are not the same thing, and machine output is not always ready to publish. This guide looks at where AI is useful and where it falls short in translation, localization and market research, and how to approach Ministry of Trade support. For general use cases, see our guide to [AI use cases in e-commerce](/rehber/e-ticarette-yapay-zeka-kullanim-alanlari/).

## Where AI helps in e-export

Three areas stand out. First, quickly producing a first translation draft of product names, descriptions and feature lists. Second, generating ideas and alternative wording to adapt that draft to the target market's language and search habits. Third, speeding up preliminary research on the target market. In all three, AI plays a supporting role as a drafting tool; the final decision belongs to someone who knows the market and the product. For detailed examples of writing product copy, see our guide to [AI-assisted product descriptions and SEO](/rehber/yapay-zeka-ile-urun-aciklamasi-ve-seo/).

## The difference between translation and localization

According to a Turkish guide for exporters, translating product names from a dictionary is not enough; sellers need to use the term buyers actually search for in their own market. The same guide says sites opened with machine translation can leave buyers with an impression of carelessness. It also covers technical points such as language-based URL structures and hreflang tags ([Monolithworks](https://www.monolithworks.com.tr/blog/ihracatci-icin-cok-dilli-kurumsal-web-sitesi/)).

In practice, localization involves questions like these:

- What name and keywords do buyers use for this product in their own language?
- Do currency, date and measurement formats match local habits?
- Are idioms, examples and cultural references understandable to the other side?
- Is the text on images also in the target language?

Machine translation does not answer these questions on its own. Stating the target market, buyer profile and tone expectations clearly in your prompt can improve results; our guide on [how to write a good prompt](/rehber/iyi-istem-nasil-yazilir/) can help. However, the research below shows that this has limits too.

## The limits of machine translation

A study published at the Machine Translation Summit 2025 reports that when translating culture-specific elements, ChatGPT used a localization strategy 16.5% of the time, while human translators did so 40.4% of the time. The study is based on literary and social-cultural texts, so its results should not be applied directly to product pages. Still, the direction is clear: machine translation tends to fall back on word-for-word translation where cultural adaptation is needed ([ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2025.mtsummit-1.36.pdf)).

The same study notes a practical constraint: Google Translate has a 5,000-character limit and ChatGPT has a 4,096-character limit, and the researchers split their text into segments of about 600 words. When translating long product descriptions or user manuals, you may need to split the text into sections and check consistency afterward.

An article covered by Slator argues that prompt engineering does not reliably improve translation performance in large language models. The study tested six models and the Czech–Ukrainian, German–English and English–Chinese language pairs ([Slator](https://slator.com/study-finds-prompt-engineering-has-limits-in-ai-translation/)). So writing prompts carefully may help, but it does not replace human control. A multimodal localization study presented at the same conference also shows that human post-editing is needed for accuracy and suitability ([ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/events/mtsummit-2025/)).

Another risk is that models may generate fabricated information. Especially in areas with legal or safety consequences, such as technical specifications, measurements, materials and certification information, compare the translation side by side with the source text. For general translation tools, see our guide to [AI translation](/rehber/yapay-zeka-ile-ceviri/).

## How to use AI for market research

The common emphasis in exporter guides is that knowing which word buyers use in their own market comes before translation. Here, AI assistants can serve as a starting point for summarizing competitor listings in the target market, generating lists of possible search terms and comparing category structures. However, do not use market size, demand and competition figures as the model produces them; such information may be fabricated. Verify figures against official statistics, the marketplace's own data or reliable reports. For methods and prompt examples, see our guide to [AI-assisted market research](/rehber/yapay-zeka-ile-pazar-arastirmasi/).

## Marketplaces' own tools

According to Amazon's Global Selling guide, the Build International Listings tool lets sellers quickly publish their listings in international stores using machine translation. In the same guide, Amazon recommends its Service Provider Network to sellers looking for translators ([Amazon Global Selling](https://sell.amazon.com/blog/amazon-listing-translation)). If speed matters, automatic translation can be a good starting point, but it makes sense to have a native speaker review the titles and descriptions of your best-selling products.

A seller blog reports that Amazon also translates text in product images using AI and that this is live in 14 marketplaces ([ZonWizard](https://www.zonwizard.com/blog/amazon-translates-product-images-with-ai-what-changes-for-international-sellers)). This information comes from a secondary source. Check in your seller account whether such a feature is active in your store, and make sure the translated text on your images looks correct. For other marketplace-focused use cases, see our guide to [AI for marketplace sellers](/rehber/pazaryeri-saticilari-icin-yapay-zeka/).

## Ministry of Trade e-export support

The Ministry of Trade's e-export consortium page includes the E-Export Consortium Status Circular dated April 13, 2026, along with a status application form ([T.C. Ticaret Bakanlığı](https://ticaret.gov.tr/destekler/e-ihracat-destekleri/e-ihracat-konsorsiyumu)). Support is administered under the E-Export Support Decision No. 5986, which covers items such as digital marketplace promotion, order fulfillment, marketplace integration and marketplace commissions ([ShipEntegra](https://www.shipentegra.com/blog/e-ihracat-e-ticaret-devlet-destekleri)).

Support rates, annual ceilings and target country lists are updated frequently. Blogs and consulting sites publish conflicting figures on this topic. So before making a budget plan, rely only on the current circular and forms published by the ministry. Also confirm with the ministry or a qualified consultant which support item, if any, could cover your AI translation and localization spending; this guide does not offer a view on that.

## How to get started

1. **Choose a small pilot.** Start with a few of your best-selling or highest-margin products.
2. **Prepare a terminology glossary.** Create a single list of preferred terms for your brand name, product features and frequently used phrases, and include this list in your translation tools and prompts.
3. **Produce a draft with machine translation.** Split long texts into sections and compare each section with the source text.
4. **Have a native speaker review it.** Prioritize the title, the first paragraph and search terms.
5. **Measure and repeat.** Track clicks, add-to-cart rates and return reasons, and add problematic phrases to your glossary.

Also decide in advance which data may be uploaded to AI tools within your company. Our guide to [company AI policy](/rehber/sirket-ici-yapay-zeka-politikasi/) can help you set those rules.
