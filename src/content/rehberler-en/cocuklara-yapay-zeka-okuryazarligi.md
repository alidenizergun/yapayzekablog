Children often encounter AI not through a lesson, but through a phone, a game, or a chatbot. That makes literacy a broader task than showing them how to use a tool: it means knowing what the tool does, where it makes mistakes, and when it should not be used. This guide draws on relevant international frameworks to explain how parents and teachers can start in practice.

## What is AI literacy?

According to the European Commission's announcement, the AILit framework sets out the basic knowledge, skills, and attitudes students need to understand and use AI systems with confidence and in a critical way ([European Commission: School Education Platform](https://school-education.ec.europa.eu/en/discover/news/give-your-feedback-new-ai-literacy-framework)). Note the three elements in this definition: knowledge (how the system works), skills (how to use it), and attitude (when it can be trusted and when it cannot).

In short, the goal is not to make children fans or enemies of AI, but to raise informed users and questioning citizens.

## The OECD and EU AILit framework

AILit is a joint initiative of the European Commission and the OECD, and was developed for primary and secondary education. The framework was jointly published by the two organizations in June 2026 ([European Commission](https://education.ec.europa.eu/node/3537)). It is built on four areas: engaging with AI, creating with AI, managing AI, and designing AI ([AI Literacy Framework](https://ailiteracyframework.org/)). The framework also contributes to the innovative domain of PISA 2029.

These four areas can be translated into everyday language for children:

- **Engaging:** Talking to AI, getting responses from it, and evaluating those responses.
- **Creating:** Producing text, images, or ideas together with AI, and knowing what is still your own work.
- **Managing:** Deciding when to use it, when to stop, and what information not to share.
- **Designing:** Having an opinion on how the technology should be built.

The framework includes learner expectations and learning scenarios for each competency, and it offers ready-made classroom examples for primary and lower secondary students ([European Commission: Empowering learners for the age of AI](https://school-education.ec.europa.eu/en/discover/publications/empowering-learners-age-ai)). The draft version counted 22 competencies, but we do not give a final number in this guide because we were unable to verify it. We also do not know whether a Turkish version of the framework exists; English, French, and German versions have been published.

## UNESCO's student competency framework

UNESCO's framework for students brings together 12 competencies across four dimensions: human-centred mindset, AI ethics, AI techniques and applications, and AI system design ([UNESCO](https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/ai-competency-framework-students)). The framework emphasizes the critical evaluation of AI solutions. Competencies progress across three levels: understand, apply, and create ([UNESCO AI Competency Framework for Students](https://policycommons.net/artifacts/16985545/ai-competency-framework-for-students/17872079/)).

These three levels are a useful skeleton for deciding the order of learning at home or in the classroom. First, the child should understand what AI is; then try a simple application; and finally create something small or discuss a design decision. Skipping steps, such as having children produce work before they understand it, risks making them dependent on the tool.

The two frameworks complement each other: AILit looks at classroom scenarios and areas, while UNESCO looks at competency dimensions and progression levels. What they share is the principle that humans remain the decision-makers.

## Turkey's Ministry of Education policy paper

The Policy Document and Action Plan on Artificial Intelligence in Education (2025-2029) of the Ministry of National Education (MEB) entered into force on June 17, 2025. The document consists of four main goals, 15 policy steps, and 40 action steps. According to the ministry's announcement, the content of existing and elective courses will be updated on the curriculum side in line with algorithmic thinking, AI technologies, and ethical principles ([MEB](https://www.meb.gov.tr/egitimde-yapay-zeka-politika-belgesi-ve-eylem-plani-yururluge-girdi/haber/37531/tr)).

We have not been able to verify reports about new AI-specific courses from an official source, so we do not provide details here. The most reliable way to learn what is being implemented at your child's school is to ask their teacher and school administration. For school-level rules, see our guide to [AI policy in schools](/rehber/okullarda-yapay-zeka-politikasi/), and for teachers, see our guide to [AI for teachers](/rehber/ogretmenler-icin-yapay-zeka/).

## How to choose age-appropriate activities

We did not find a definitive age threshold in official frameworks; most online age charts are based on blog posts. For this reason, the suggestions below are not an age chart, but examples derived from the logic of the frameworks above. Adapt them to the child's development and interests.

- **Understanding (starting point for younger children):** Show the child a moment when an app makes a recommendation, and ask why that recommendation was made. Screen-free games also work: you secretly set a rule, and the child guesses the rule from examples. This prepares the idea that systems learn from examples.
- **Applying (middle school and beyond):** Asking the same question in two different ways and comparing the answers shows how the wording of a prompt changes the result. While doing this, you can draw on ideas from our guide to [how to write a good prompt](/rehber/iyi-istem-nasil-yazilir/).
- **Creating and discussing (older children):** Have them prepare a draft with AI and write down what they changed and what they cut, and why. UNESCO's framework supports working through concepts such as human autonomy at the high school level using case studies and discussions; you can open a similar discussion at home.

Repeat the same three questions in every activity: Who or what produced this output? How do we know that? Who makes the decision? These questions are the everyday version of AILit's four areas.

## Critical thinking and verification habits

According to a global AI report, critical thinking depends on understanding when a tool is useful and how it works ([Global Index on Responsible AI: 2026 Report](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.14782)). In other words, critical thinking does not mean viewing everything with suspicion; it means using the tool in the right place and knowingly.

We can suggest a practical loop for verification. This is not a method from any institution's framework, but a general practice:

1. Choose a claim from the AI's response.
2. Search for the same information in a textbook, an encyclopedia, or another reliable source.
3. If the two sources conflict, consult a third source or an adult.
4. Record the result: Was the AI correct, incomplete, or wrong?

This is how a child sees, through concrete examples, that chatbots can give fluent but wrong answers (fabricated information, or [hallucination](/sozluk/halusinasyon/)). You can turn catching errors into a game: whoever finds a mistake earns a point. Visual and audio content also requires similar scrutiny; our guide on [how to spot deepfakes](/rehber/deepfake-nasil-anlasilir/) offers a starting point.

## Risks, limits, and where to start

The same global report warns that pedagogical strategies suited to children have not yet received enough attention. The report also states that effective implementation is not possible without guidance for teachers who have heavy workloads and limited resources ([Global Index on Responsible AI: 2026 Report](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.14782)). This shows that responsibility cannot be placed solely on parents or teachers.

A few practical limits:

- **Supervision:** Young children should use AI tools together with an adult. Check the age requirements of a tool in advance.
- **Personal data:** Teach children from the very beginning that they should not enter information such as their name, school, address, or photos into tools. For details, see our guide to [protecting personal data when using AI](/rehber/yapay-zeka-kullanirken-kisisel-verileri-koruma/).
- **Dependence:** Do not let the tool become the one who does the homework; let the child try first, then compare.
- **Bias:** Tools can carry patterns from their training data; identify stereotypes in the output together.

Where should you start? Choose a single half-hour activity this week, ask the three questions above, and talk through the results. For a broader view aimed at parents, our [AI guide for parents](/rehber/veliler-icin-yapay-zeka-rehberi/) will be useful. Small, regular steps tend to stick better than one large, one-time training.
