AI has largely made its way into the daily work of marketing teams, but how much of this use actually produces results is still debated. This guide summarizes the tasks where teams use AI most, a reasonable example workflow for each, and what the research says. The example workflows are general implementation suggestions and are not based on a specific study or tool. Most research findings come from secondary reports, so read the figures as directional indicators.

## What the research says about adoption

Salesforce's 10th State of Marketing study is based on responses from 4,450 marketing decision-makers in North America, Latin America, Asia-Pacific and Europe. The survey ran from October 8 to November 17, 2025, using a double-blind method ([Salesforce](https://www.salesforce.com/news/stories/state-of-marketing-2026/)). [Stratcom Academy](https://stratcom.academy/2026/03/23/ai-adoption-is-high-but-marketing-execution-still-falls-short-according-to-2026-salesforce-state-of-marketing-report/), which covered the report, writes that 75% of marketers have adopted AI, but mostly to grow scale rather than to improve relevance. According to Salesforce, the most common use is personalized content creation.

In HubSpot's 2026 study of more than 1,500 marketers, 86.4% of teams say they use AI in at least a few marketing areas. In terms of usage intensity, content creation stands at 42.5%, media production at 37.2%, and administrative task automation at 35.6% ([HubSpot](https://blog.hubspot.com/marketing/hubspot-blog-marketing-industry-trends-report)). In HubSpot's separate State of AI in Marketing study, 98% of 1,754 marketers report using AI in some form, but only 10% say they have reached a "transformative" level of maturity ([HubSpot](https://blog.hubspot.com/marketing/state-of-generative-ai)).

## Content and text creation

Content is the task where AI is used most intensively. A simple workflow could look like this: first, write a clear prompt that includes the topic, target audience and source notes; ask the model for draft and headline options; then edit the draft by hand to match brand tone and real data; finally, publish with an editor's approval. Keep in mind that the model can generate fabricated information (hallucinations), so always check figures, names and quotes against their sources. For prompt writing, see the [guide to writing good prompts](/rehber/iyi-istem-nasil-yazilir/). To keep your brand voice consistent, see the [guide to content creation and brand voice](/rehber/yapay-zeka-ile-icerik-uretimi-ve-marka-sesi/).

The central warning in the Salesforce report applies here: production is speeding up, but content may not be becoming more relevant to readers. The job of the team is to turn the speed gains into the time it spends making content more original.

## Visuals and video

According to HubSpot, media production ranks second in intensive use, after content (37.2%). An example workflow: write the campaign idea and visual guidelines (colors, style, elements to avoid); generate several image or short video drafts; discard those that do not fit the brand; and publish the selected one after a final touch-up by your designer. AI speeds up the draft stage, but brand consistency and detail errors (such as distorted hands or misspelled text) require a human eye. To get started, see the [guide to creating images with AI](/rehber/yapay-zeka-ile-gorsel-olusturma/). The [best tools by task](/en-iyi/) page can help you find a tool that fits your work, but the research notes contain no verified finding that any specific tool is superior.

## Ad copy variations

Ad copy is one of the tasks that suits AI naturally: it is short, repetitive and measurable. The workflow: define a single offer and core message; ask the model for many headlines and descriptions using different emphases (benefit, urgency, social proof); remove anything that breaks the channel's character limits or advertising policies; and A/B test the rest, recording the results. AI generates variations; data shows which one works. For the AI features built into advertising platforms, see the guide to AI in ad platforms.

## Customer research and reporting

Summarizing and grouping free-text material such as survey responses, support tickets and reviews is a common use. An example workflow: give the model data with personal information removed; ask it to extract themes and sample sentences; read a few records yourself to verify the themes it produced; and carry the findings into your report. A similar approach works for reporting: AI writes the first draft of a weekly performance note, and an analyst checks the figures.

Data is decisive here. [diginomica](https://diginomica.com/salesforces-state-marketing-ai-dominates-agenda-data-still-holds-marketers-back), which covered the Salesforce study, reports that 98% of marketing teams using AI face at least one data-related personalization obstacle. Messy and unreliable data does not produce useful summaries, so organize your data first.

## Email personalization

According to the Salesforce study, 78% of marketers need more personalized content than they can produce ([Adin](https://www.adin.ai/marketing-radar/reports/library/state-of-marketing-10th-edition-2026/)). Email is where this need is most visible. An example workflow: divide your audience into a few meaningful segments; write one core message for each segment; ask AI to adapt that message to the segment's needs; and test it with a small group before sending. Simply changing the name and sending the same text is exactly what Salesforce criticizes as "one-way, generic campaigns."

Wherever you use personal data in a workflow, pay attention to consent and data protection rules. For details, see the guide to personalization and KVKK (Turkey's Personal Data Protection Authority).

## Risks and limits

Use is widespread, but maturity is low. [Financial Marketer](https://financial-marketer.com/mckinseys-state-of-marketing-2026-brand-is-back-but-ai-is-creating-a-new-marketing-divide/), which reports on McKinsey's European marketing research, writes that 94% of marketing organizations in Europe have only low or medium generative AI capabilities. According to the same source, 90% of CMOs are testing use cases, but fewer than 10% have been able to scale them across workflows.

On the broader business side, the picture is also cautious. [Magica](https://magica.com/news/mckinsey-state-of-ai-2026-enterprise-roi), which covers McKinsey's AI research, reports that the share of respondents attributing a positive contribution of AI to EBIT remained at 37%, and that revenue gains were seen most in marketing and sales. On productivity, [a HubSpot report](https://crossglobemarketing.com/blogs/hubspot-state-of-ai-marketing-trends-2026/) says 26.5% of marketers saw a noticeable increase in productivity, and 66.2% reported a slight or moderate increase. So there are gains, but they are mostly modest.

Agent-based systems are not yet widespread: the Salesforce study says only 13% use agentic AI. Before granting autonomous permissions to agents, it is worth reading the [guide to using AI agents safely](/rehber/yapay-zeka-ajanlarini-guvenle-kullanma/).

## Where to start

- Choose one task, such as email subject line variations or a weekly report draft.
- Write your success metric upfront: time spent, click-through rate or number of revisions, for example.
- Have a person approve every output before publication, and check figures and claims against their sources.
- Record what worked and what did not in a short note, and build a shared prompt library and checklist for the team.
- If your data is messy, organize it before moving on to personalization.

The common message of the research is that AI is a widely used but still immature tool in marketing. Speed is easy to gain; the real difference lies in your ability to turn the time you save into more relevant and original work.
