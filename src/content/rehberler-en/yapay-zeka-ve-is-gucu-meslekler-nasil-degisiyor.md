Both gloomy and overly optimistic headlines are circulating about AI's impact on the workforce. For HR teams, the more practical questions are: which tasks are changing, which skills are in demand, and how can employees prepare for the transition? This guide summarizes the findings published by the World Economic Forum (WEF), the IMF, the OECD and Anthropic, along with their sources, without overstating them. Most figures here are estimates or depend on measurement methods, so they should not be read as firm predictions of the future.

## Start by separating what the numbers measure

The concept most often confused in these debates is "exposure." When a job is exposed to AI, some of its tasks can be performed with AI. This does not mean the job will disappear. In its [October 2026 article](https://www.imf.org/en/blogs/articles/2026/10/15/the-world-cant-afford-to-get-ai-wrong), the IMF also says some jobs are being supplemented by AI, while others may be affected through replacement.

The scope of institutions also differs: one covers global jobs, another advanced economies, and another employer surveys. For that reason, do not add up percentages from different sources or compare them directly. Also, the data in this guide is at the level of global or country groups. Rather than assuming it applies directly to Türkiye, it is more sound to look at the task structure of your own sector and company.

## WEF: new roles are growing, some roles are fading

[The Future of Jobs Report 2025](https://www.weforum.org/publications/the-future-of-jobs-report-2025) is based on views from more than 1,000 employers across 55 economies, covering 22 industry clusters and representing more than 14 million workers in total. According to the report, the fastest-growing roles in percentage terms are technology-driven: big data specialists, fintech engineers, AI and machine learning specialists, and software and application developers.

According to a [WEF article](https://www.weforum.org/stories/2025/01/future-of-jobs-report-2025-the-fastest-growing-and-declining-jobs/) looking at the overall picture, AI and information processing will create 11 million roles by 2030 while eliminating 9 million. Robots and automation will cause a net loss of 5 million. The [WEF's 2026 summary](https://www.weforum.org/stories/2026/01/top-jobs-and-labour-market-stories-2025/) reports that 78 million net new roles will emerge by 2030, while 22% of existing jobs will undergo structural change.

Employer attitudes also matter for HR. According to the report, about half of employers plan to restructure their operations around AI, two-thirds want to hire workers with AI skills, and 40% expect to reduce headcount in areas where AI can take on tasks. In skills, AI and big data are the fastest-growing area, followed by networks and cybersecurity, and technological literacy. Creative thinking, resilience and lifelong learning are also among the leading competencies.

## IMF: high exposure, but the impact is in the details

The IMF's [January 2026 article](https://www.imf.org/en/blogs/articles/2026/01/14/new-skills-and-ai-are-reshaping-the-future-of-work) states that about 40% of global jobs are exposed to change driven by AI. The [October 2026 article](https://www.imf.org/en/blogs/articles/2026/10/15/the-world-cant-afford-to-get-ai-wrong) says this share rises to about 60% in advanced economies. The same article notes that the unemployment impact is not yet visible, but the nature of work is changing.

Three findings stand out for HR teams:

- In Asia-Pacific, employer demand for the occupations most exposed to AI has fallen by about 10%. At the same time, demand is rising for workers who can get more done using AI. New roles such as prompt engineering and AI agent development are emerging.
- Exposure is higher for entry-level jobs. According to the January 2026 article, the first-rung jobs of young and recent graduates are a more vulnerable area in this respect.
- Demand for new skills is growing. According to the same article, in advanced economies one in every 10 online job postings requests at least one new skill, while in emerging economies it is one in 20.

## OECD: transformation and training

According to the OECD's [Skills in the AI age](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/skills-in-the-ai-age_972bd15e-en/full-report/component-4.html) summary dated July 8, 2026, AI is transforming jobs more than destroying them, and evidence often shows it complementing human labor. However, the risk of job losses persists in routine and repetitive roles.

The OECD's [AI and skills](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/ai-and-skills_f843b352-en/full-report.html) policy note, dated June 5, 2026, addresses two points directly relevant to HR. First, more than half of workers who use AI report receiving employer-funded training, and those who were trained view AI's impact on performance and working conditions more positively. Second, employers say AI is increasing demand for highly educated workers.

## Anthropic Economic Index: where usage is concentrated

Anthropic's [March 2026 report](https://www.anthropic.com/research/economic-index-march-2026-report), with data from February 2026, looks at real usage data. According to it, Claude usage is concentrated in high-income countries and in US states with high shares of knowledge workers. Usage also focuses on a small number of specialized tasks.

This data reflects the usage of a single company's product and should not be read as AI usage across the whole sector. Still, it aligns with the broader picture that specific tasks change more than entire occupations.

## Practical steps for HR teams

To turn these findings into action, you can start with:

1. **Look at tasks, not titles.** List the tasks for each role and separate repetitive ones from those requiring judgment and relationships. This way, you ask "which part of this role changes?" instead of "does this role disappear?"
2. **Plan training.** The OECD finding shows that training also shapes employees' views of AI. For a detailed framework, see our guide to AI training for employees.
3. **Put rules in writing.** It should be clear which data can be entered into AI tools and who will verify the outputs. A company AI policy is a useful starting point.
4. **Review entry-level roles.** The high exposure the IMF highlights suggests that tasks in internship and graduate programs may need to be redesigned. This is an inference; test it with your own data.
5. **Make skills concrete in job postings.** Instead of vague phrases like "AI knowledge," state which tool you expect to be used for which task and for what purpose. For candidates, the guide to [preparing a CV with AI](/rehber/yapay-zeka-ile-cv-hazirlama/) may also help.

At the individual level, starting small is enough: pick one repetitive task in your daily work and try it with AI. The guide on [where to start learning AI](/rehber/yapay-zeka-ogrenmeye-nereden-baslamali/) offers a step-by-step path. For other sector-specific uses, see the [AI in human resources](/rehber/insan-kaynaklarinda-yapay-zeka/) page.

## Risks and limits

Keep a few limits in mind when using this data:

- **Forecasts are not certainties.** The WEF's 2030 figures are based on employer expectations, and actual outcomes may differ.
- **Averages don't describe individuals.** A rate for a sector or country group does not determine the situation at your company or in your role.
- **A new role does not mean an automatic transition.** As new roles emerge, moving existing employees into them requires training, time and planning. Even the WEF's specific figures do not remove the difficulty of this transition.
- **Human decisions are essential on sensitive matters.** If you use AI in hiring and performance evaluation, address bias and personal data risks separately, and do not upload employee data into tools without rules.

In short, the shared message of reliable sources is this: AI is mostly transforming jobs, putting pressure on some routine tasks, and giving an advantage to employees who are open to learning. The job of HR teams is to turn this transformation into a plan covering tasks, skills and training.
