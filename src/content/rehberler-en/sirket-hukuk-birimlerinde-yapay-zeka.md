In-house legal teams have started testing AI for tasks ranging from contract review to compliance tracking. However, survey data shows that more usage does not mean regular governance or measurement. This guide outlines where adoption stands, where AI helps in contract lifecycle management, what to watch for in vendor contracts, and which questions to ask when choosing a vendor. It is general information, not legal advice; consult a qualified lawyer for a specific situation.

## How widespread is use?

Treat survey results with caution. Most sources are survey firms, software providers or law firms, and each uses a different sample. The direction is nonetheless consistent.

According to a survey by the Association of Corporate Counsel (ACC), [85% of in-house legal departments are using AI](https://inhousecommunityeu.com/ai-use-reaches-85-of-in-house-legal-departments-acc-survey-finds/). The rate is reported as 23% in 2024 and 52% in 2025. The survey had 576 respondents and data was collected in July 2026.

The governance findings stand out most. According to the same report, 39% of departments do not formally track the return on their AI investments, and 41% have never audited unauthorized AI tool use (shadow AI). In 73% of cases, the final approval for AI tools rests outside the legal department, mostly with IT management. The legal team may be using the tool, but lawyers do not always decide which tools are chosen or on what terms.

## What is changing in contract review?

Contract work is the area where legal teams are looking at AI most closely. According to a survey of 452 people by LegalOn and In-House Connect, [52% of in-house teams are using or evaluating AI for contract review](https://www.legalontech.com/resources/2026-state-of-ai-for-in-house-legal). Eighty-seven percent of respondents believe AI will help with contract review and redlining. In the same survey, reviewing a contract takes an average of 3.1 hours, so the potential time savings are meaningful.

Interest in AI agents is also growing. In the same survey, 80% of teams are examining or evaluating agents, but most prefer automation that a person supervises. That preference makes sense, because generative AI can produce fabricated information. The risk is even greater in tasks such as case law and legislation research beyond contracts; for more, see our guide to [the risk of fabricated case law](/rehber/hukukta-yapay-zeka-uydurma-ictihat-riski/). For practical details on contracts, the [AI in contract review](/rehber/sozlesme-incelemesinde-yapay-zeka/) guide is also useful.

## Contract lifecycle management (CLM) and compliance tracking

Contract lifecycle management (CLM) refers to software that manages every stage of a contract, from drafting and signing to monitoring and renewal. The findings on AI's contribution in this area are as follows:

- In a 2026 study by Deloitte and DocuSign, [more than 1,100 leaders reported 36% efficiency gains from AI-powered contract workflows](https://www.deloitte.com/us/en/services/tax/articles/ai-contract-lifecycle-management-survey.html). Of organizations not using AI for contract management, 95% are considering or planning to do so.
- In a survey of 250 people by Conga with Talker Research, [95% of organizations using CLM are using AI, but only 24% describe their CLM processes as optimized](https://conga.com/resources/clm-ai-2026-trend-report). Usage is concentrated in search, reporting and risk assessment, and 92% of respondents require human review of AI outputs.

These figures suggest that AI in CLM is widespread but not yet mature. For compliance tracking, its practical value lies in finding and reporting specific obligations, dates and clauses across hundreds of contracts. For such tasks, AI offers a fast first screen, but the lawyer at the company that is party to the contract makes the decision.

A simple recommendation: before a major investment, check whether your contract archive is complete, current and searchable. No tool produces good results from a disorganized or unscanned archive.

## How to get started

Starting with a small, measurable pilot limits both risk and cost:

1. **Choose one task.** For example, a recurring contract type such as standard non-disclosure agreements.
2. **Define success metrics upfront.** Since 39% of departments in the ACC data do not track AI return on investment, setting metrics such as time and error rates at the outset gives you an advantage.
3. **Make human review part of the process.** Document who approves outputs and at which stage.
4. **Create an internal policy.** Clearly state which data may enter which tool. [Our guide to a company AI policy](/rehber/sirket-ici-yapay-zeka-politikasi/) offers a framework.
5. **Bring legal, IT and information security into the decision.** Where approval mostly sits with IT, it is important for the legal team to put contract terms on the table in advance.

## AI clauses in vendor contracts

When contracting with a vendor that offers AI, a standard software agreement is often not enough. According to an article published by Legal 500, in practice [AI provisions are usually set out in a separate addendum](https://www.legal500.com/intelligence/united-states/technology/ai-addendums-contract-clauses-to-negotiate-with-ai-vendors). The addendum governs how the provider's AI systems may use customer data, who owns the outputs, and the audit obligations the provider accepts.

The same source recommends at least the following: a training ban (your data may not be used to train the model), output ownership, logging and audit trails, human oversight, AI-specific indemnification, and protection against prompt injection. The article also includes a warning: a provider may agree not to use data for training while reserving rights for optimization or quality assurance. For this reason, read the scope and exceptions to the training ban line by line.

A practice guide on model changes recommends [notice of significant changes, transparent explanation of their impact, and a right to suspend or terminate in the event of risky changes](https://www.mnlegal.net/insights/ai-vendor-contracts-key-clauses-to-demand-in-2026). The same source notes that if the provider processes personal data on your behalf, a data processing clause is needed covering purpose, security, sub-processors, international transfers and deletion obligations.

For the EU AI Act, it is also important to define roles clearly in the contract. According to Vircon Legal's guide, [a written role definition can prevent a customer from inadvertently becoming a provider](https://virconlegal.com/tr/yapay-zeka-tedarikci-sozlesmesi-maddeler-rehberi/). For how the law affects companies in Turkey, see the [EU AI Act and Turkey](/rehber/ab-yapay-zeka-yasasi-ve-turkiye/) guide. The law's implementation timeline should also be kept current, so confirm it with your legal advisor.

## Questions to ask when choosing a vendor

A checklist compiled from Turkish KVKK (Turkey's Personal Data Protection Law) sources can be summarized as follows:

- **Can a data processing agreement be signed, and what does it cover?** A KVKK compliance article recommends this as the first step in vendor evaluation.
- **Are free or individual service terms adequate for corporate use?** [According to a KVKK article](https://www.securefors.com/is-yerinde-golge-yapay-zeka-shadow-ai-kvkk-riskleri/), gaps persist when public tools such as ChatGPT or Gemini are used by companies without moving to a corporate version and signing a data processing agreement.
- **Is data used for training, and are retention and deletion commitments included?** It is recommended that [these issues be stated explicitly in the contract](https://www.yapayzekayazilimlari.com/blog/kvkk-uyumlu-yapay-zeka).
- **Is cloud hosted abroad, and what is the transfer mechanism?** According to the same source, entering personal data into a cloud service hosted abroad may count as a transfer under Article 9 of KVKK. An adequacy decision, a safeguard such as standard contractual clauses, or an exception is required.
- **Does the provider offer a high-risk system, and what is your role under the EU AI Act?** The role should be written into the contract.
- **What are the incident notification deadlines?** Tie data breach notification periods to the contract and align them with your obligations under KVKK.

For a more detailed view of personal data risks, see our guide to [KVKK and AI tools](/rehber/kvkk-ve-yapay-zeka-araclari/).

## Risks and limits

- **Survey bias.** Many sources come from vendors that sell products. Do not accept efficiency figures without measuring them in your own company.
- **Getting stuck in pilots.** One industry survey reports that most organizations that have adopted AI in some form have not moved to wide-scale deployment. Starting small and measuring is therefore realistic.
- **Output rights.** According to Pitch Law, output produced entirely by AI may not receive copyright protection. For this reason, output rights and indemnification against third-party intellectual property claims should be addressed separately in the contract.
- **Shadow AI.** When no approved tool is available, employees may turn to their own tools. Offering an approved option and announcing the policy is more effective than simply banning tools.
