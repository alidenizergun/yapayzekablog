Generative AI (ÜYZ) tools have entered many steps of the academic workflow, from literature searches to copy editing. They can speed things up, but the researcher always has the final say and bears responsibility for the scientific work. This guide covers literature tools, using AI in writing, the risk of fabricated sources, journal and peer review rules, and the YÖK ethics guide within a practical framework. Rules vary from institution to institution and from journal to journal, so at every step consult the current text from your own university and your target journal.

## What to expect from AI in a literature review

Tools such as Elicit, Consensus and [NotebookLM](/arac/notebooklm/) are offered to help you find studies on a topic, summarize long texts and compare sources. How these tools work, which databases they search and how reliable they are can differ. So rather than treating a tool as the "source of truth," it is healthier to think of it as a screening assistant that points you toward papers to read.

A practical starting point: write your research question in one clear sentence, pull the list of papers the tool suggests, then open each one from the publisher's or database's page. Base your work on the paper itself, not on its summary. Keep in mind that summaries can oversimplify methodological details, sample limits and caveats. When working with long PDFs, our [guide to summarizing PDFs and long documents with AI](/rehber/yapay-zeka-ile-pdf-ve-uzun-belge-ozetleme/) and [our guide to writing good prompts](/rehber/iyi-istem-nasil-yazilir/) may help.

## The risk of fabricated sources

Large language models can generate article titles, authors or citation details that do not exist. This is called hallucination, meaning the production of fabricated information. A fabricated citation often looks convincing: a familiar journal name, a plausible year, a real author's name. The problem lies precisely in that credibility. Even in tools that cite sources, you must separately verify that the cited source actually supports the claim.

To build a practical verification habit, follow these steps:

- Search for each source by its title or DOI number in an independent database.
- If you find the source, check whether the information you are citing is actually in that paper.
- Correct author, year, volume and page details against the original record.
- Do not include any source in your text that you cannot verify.

An error in the bibliography is counted as your error, even if it came from AI.

## AI in writing: where it helps and where the limits are

Tasks such as language correction, simplifying draft text, generating title alternatives and smoothing out a paragraph written in a foreign language are relatively low-risk uses. By contrast, having AI write the study's original contribution, interpret your findings or draw conclusions raises problems for both scientific originality and accountability. An AI tool cannot take responsibility as an author; you are the one who answers for a wrong sentence.

Another limit is data. Before pasting unpublished findings, participant data or materials containing personal information into a publicly available chatbot, stop and think. [Our guide on KVKK (Turkey's Personal Data Protection Authority) and AI tools](/rehber/kvkk-ve-yapay-zeka-araclari/) offers a basic framework on this. In studies that require ethics committee approval, the scope of any data sharing must also match the committee's decision.

## What the YÖK ethics guide says

The Council of Higher Education (YÖK) has prepared an ethics guide on the use of generative AI in the research and publishing activities of higher education institutions. Sources give different dates for when the guide was announced: [Istanbul Okan University's news item](https://www.okan.edu.tr/ogrenme-merkezi/haber/3568/yok-ve-tubitak-yapay-zeka-etik-rehberi-yayimladi/) gives May 10, 2024, while [Çanakkale Onsekiz Mart University's announcement](https://sbf.comu.edu.tr/arsiv/duyurular/yokten-yapay-zeka-kullanimina-dair-etik-rehber-r1720.html) gives May 13, 2024. For the exact date, rely on the official text.

[According to Anadolu Agency](https://www.aa.com.tr/tr/egitim/yokten-uretken-yapay-zeka-kullanimina-dair-etik-rehber/3212359), the guide is built around four main sections. Its core principles include transparency, honesty, care, fairness and respect, confidentiality, and accountability, while risks and ethical issues and recommendations are addressed under separate headings. You can translate these principles into your own workflow as follows: if you used AI, say so clearly (transparency); verify the outputs yourself (care and honesty); do not move data into unprotected environments (confidentiality); and remember that you are responsible for the entire text (accountability).

How the guide is applied at your university also matters. Many universities issue their own directives or principles, so check whether your institute has additional requirements for thesis and project processes. For broader context, see [our guide on AI and academic integrity at universities](/rehber/universitelerde-yapay-zeka-ve-akademik-durustluk/).

## Journal and publisher policies

AI rules set by journals and publishers are not uniform. Some journals require that AI use be disclosed in the text, while others restrict certain types of use. Rules for image and figure generation may differ further. Before submitting, read the AI section of the journal's author guidelines; if there is no such section, send a brief email to the editor to ask.

In practice, do the following:

- Keep a notebook recording which tools you used, for what purpose and at which stage.
- If the journal asks for a disclosure, write it in the methods, acknowledgments or a separate statement, in the format the journal requires.
- Do not try to hide your AI use. If it comes to light later, the discovery creates a bigger trust problem than the use itself.
- If you are unsure about copyright or image rights, review [our guide on AI and copyright](/rehber/yapay-zeka-ve-telif-hakki/).

## AI in peer review

An article sent to you as a reviewer is unpublished and confidential. Uploading this text to a publicly available AI service could put the author's work and confidentiality at risk. The confidentiality and accountability principles in the YÖK guide can also serve as a useful guide here. However, which uses are permitted and which are prohibited in peer review varies from journal to journal. The safest path is to read the wording in the invitation letter and the reviewer guidelines, and if anything is unclear, obtain written approval from the editor.

Even where a use is permitted, you are responsible for the content of your review. Do not present AI output as your own observations; your critique should reflect the judgment of someone who has actually read and understood the paper.

## A short checklist to get started

1. Read the AI rules of your university, your institute and your target journal.
2. Turn the core principles of the YÖK ethics guide into a checklist for your own work.
3. Use literature tools for screening, and read the papers yourself.
4. Verify every source and quotation against the original record.
5. Do not upload unpublished or personal data to unprotected environments.
6. Record your use and declare it clearly when required.

AI does not replace academic work; used correctly, it only eases some mechanical steps. Keeping in mind that responsibility and scientific judgment remain with you is the soundest starting point, both ethically and practically.
