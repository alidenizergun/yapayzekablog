Making AI ad images and videos can shorten production time and let you quickly test different variations. But this speed comes with risks around copyright, personality rights, labeling and brand perception. This guide summarizes, based on sourced information, what to check before you start production. It is not legal opinion; for specific campaigns, consult a lawyer.

## Where to start the workflow

Generative AI tools can produce images, video, audio and avatars from text prompts. To start, it makes sense to pick a single campaign variation and move forward with a small test. You can find the basics of image generation in the [creating AI images](/rehber/yapay-zeka-ile-gorsel-olusturma/) guide.

Before choosing a tool, check the following:

- Read the license text granting commercial use rights, and its changes.
- Check whether the tool offers any protection against training data and possible infringement claims.
- Determine who will use the output and on which channels.
- Keep records of your outputs (prompt, version, date).

License terms vary from tool to tool and are updated over time. So rather than accepting general assumptions, base your decisions on the current terms of the tool you are using.

## Brand AI ads and consumer backlash

Recent examples show that being legally compliant is not enough for brand safety. In December 2025, Valentino published a fully AI-generated campaign for its DeVain bag. Even though the ad was labeled, it was criticized on Instagram with comments calling it "cheap" and "lazy." According to the same source, Coca-Cola's AI-generated Christmas ad also drew backlash due to an eerie feeling in the visuals ([Yahoo Finance](https://finance.yahoo.com/media-advertising/articles/valentino-coca-cola-faced-fierce-100000529.html)).

Equinox, meanwhile, pulled its "Question Everything" campaign in July 2026. The campaign aimed to critique the fakeness of AI, but after Asian American content creators found one of the visuals racist and dehumanizing, the ad was taken down ([One Media Society](https://www.onemediasociety.com/journal/ai-advertising-backlash-wellness-hospitality-brands)). The lesson here is that visuals should be reviewed by human eyes before publication, through a check that covers different perspectives.

A study on luxury advertising finds that when luxury brands use AI visuals and disclose it, consumer reaction is more negative; consumers see less effort and lower brand value ([Yahoo Finance](https://finance.yahoo.com/media-advertising/articles/valentino-coca-cola-faced-fierce-100000529.html)). In other words, labeling may be necessary, but it does not prevent backlash on its own; consider separately whether it fits your brand positioning.

Usage in the industry is growing. According to an IAB report, 83% of advertising executives say their companies use AI in the creative process; in 2024, this figure was 60%. In the same research, 73% of Gen Z and millennial consumers say that knowing an ad was made with AI either increases or does not change their likelihood to purchase ([One Media Society](https://www.onemediasociety.com/journal/ai-advertising-backlash-wellness-hospitality-brands)). This suggests that disclosure may be less risky than non-disclosure in most cases.

## Copyright: who owns the output

Under FSEK (Turkey's Intellectual and Artistic Works Law), the author is the real person who creates the work. An unedited output produced from a single prompt is reportedly hard to protect by copyright; if the user makes a clear creative contribution, protection may arise ([Yavuz Hukuk Bürosu](https://www.eyavuz.av.tr/yapay-zeka-ile-uretilen-eserlerde-telif-haklari/)). In practice, this could mean your protection against a competitor copying your campaign visual may be weak. For details, see the [AI and copyright](/rehber/yapay-zeka-ve-telif-hakki/) guide.

Do not assume there is an established practice in this area; sources offer differing interpretations, and you will need to verify official institutional practice on your own.

## Logo, character and real-person risks

If the output includes a brand logo, a well-known character or a real person's face, copyright and trademark liability may arise where it creates a likelihood of confusion. If a real person's face is involved, personality rights and personal data legislation also come into play ([Av. Mert Çelik](https://avukatmertcelik.com/makaleler/dijital-hukuk/yapay-zeka-ile-uretilen-icerik-telif-ihlali-mi.html)). For this reason, if you use the image of a celebrity, employee or customer, obtain written consent. You can find legal remedies for deepfake content in the [deepfakes and law](/rehber/deepfake-ve-hukuk/) guide.

Virtual characters and avatars require similar care; see the [virtual influencers and AI avatars](/rehber/sanal-influencerlar-ve-yapay-zeka-avatarlari/) guide.

## Turkey's new advertising rules

The Ministry of Trade amended the Regulation on Commercial Advertising and Unfair Commercial Practices. The amendment was published in the Official Gazette on July 1, 2026. For AI-generated ads, it became mandatory to clearly disclose digital characters that are hard to distinguish from humans. Ads that give the impression that a real person's AI-generated digital copy has experienced or recommended a product have also been banned ([12punto](https://12punto.com.tr/gundem/ticaret-bakanligindan-reklam-yonetmeliginde-degisiklik-yapay-zeka-ile-olusturulan-reklamlara-duzenleme-143055)).

Most of the regulations are reportedly set to take effect on August 1, 2026. Coverage does not clearly separate the Official Gazette publication date from the effective date, so verify your campaign plan against the regulation text. The same regulation also made the label "reklam" (advertisement) or "tanıtım" (promotion) mandatory for influencer posts where there is a material benefit in return ([Marketing Türkiye](https://www.marketingturkiye.com.tr/haberler/ticaret-bakanligindan-yapay-zeka-influencer-ve-hedefli-reklamlara-kapsamli-duzen-hicbir-sey-eskisi-gibi-olmayacak/)).

One of the Advertising Board's (Reklam Kurulu) decisions from the July 2026 period (2026/1607) offers a hint on this. The AI apps Podit and CarPeak were presented on Instagram and YouTube as a product story that a content creator had "coincidentally discovered." The posts had no advertising, invitation or partnership label. The board ruled that this framing did not remove the advertising nature of the content or the burden of proof for claims. The same decision also treated hiding negative comments, steering people who had not made a purchase to write reviews, and artificially inflating likes, followers and view counts as manipulation of consumer perception ([Lexology](https://www.lexology.com/library/detail.aspx?g=123c65c5-331e-4b74-8caa-936e729c5e51)). For ad tools on platforms, see the [AI on advertising platforms](/rehber/reklam-platformlarinda-yapay-zeka/) guide.

## EU transparency: AI Act Article 50

If you run campaigns aimed at the EU market, Article 50 of the AI Act matters. According to the European Commission's Code of Practice page, the relevant obligations apply from August 2, 2026. Voluntary rules do not change legal obligations; the Commission has also prepared a set of icons for deployers who will label deepfake content ([European Commission](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/code-practice-ai-generated-content)).

Timing details matter: before August 2, 2026, retroactive labeling is not mandatory for content that has both been generated and published. The transition period for marking obligations for generative AI systems placed on the market before that date runs until December 2, 2026. If generated content is published after August 2, labeling is required ([Stibbe](https://www.stibbe.com/publications-and-insights/the-ai-acts-transparency-obligations-rules-scope-and-timeline)). According to the same source, the Commission published guidance on Article 50 on July 20, 2026. For the general framework, you can also see the [EU AI law and Turkey](/rehber/ab-yapay-zeka-yasasi-ve-turkiye/) guide.

## Platform labels and a practical checklist

Platforms where you buy ads (Meta, Google, TikTok, YouTube and similar) have their own AI labeling rules, which can change frequently. Check the current terms on the platform's official help page before each campaign, and do not rely solely on third-party summaries.

Before publishing, answer these questions:

- Does the ad contain a character that is hard to distinguish from a human AI character; if so, has it been clearly disclosed?
- Is a real person's digital copy shown as if they had tried or recommended the product?
- Is there a resemblance to a logo, a well-known character or a real person's face; has consent been obtained?
- Have you read the commercial use license of the tool you are using?
- Has the visual been reviewed by people from different backgrounds?
- Are your documents ready to back up your claims?
- For ads targeting the EU, have the labeling obligations and dates been checked?

Finally, document your AI use policy in your brand guidelines and share it with your team. That way, you won't have to debate the same rules from scratch in every campaign.
