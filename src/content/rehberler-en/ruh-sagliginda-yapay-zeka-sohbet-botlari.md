The number of people turning to AI chatbots for mental health support is growing. This guide summarizes clinical studies on the topic, warnings from expert organizations, the first legal restrictions in the United States and the safeguards companies have introduced. The aim is not to give you medical advice, but to show what these tools may be useful for, where their limits lie and when you should see a professional.

## Why this matters

A 2026 American Psychological Association (APA) survey titled "Chatbots and Mental Health" included 1,242 licensed psychologists. [According to the APA](https://www.apa.org/topics/artificial-intelligence-machine-learning/discussing-ai-use-therapy), 77% of psychologists said their patients were using AI, and 94% said chatbots cannot adequately treat mental health conditions. In other words, these tools are not staying outside the therapy room; clients are already using them. That makes it important to set the right expectations. For a basic framework on how a general-purpose [chatbot](/arac/chatgpt/) can be used for health questions, see the guide to [using AI for health questions](/rehber/saglik-sorularinda-yapay-zeka-kullanimi/).

## What clinical studies show

The most widely cited study is a randomized controlled trial of Therabot, a generative AI-based chatbot. [According to its PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42932588/), the study was published in the journal NEJM AI on March 27, 2025. Over four weeks, 210 adults were assigned either to a group using Therabot (106 people) or to a waitlist control group (104 people). The authors say this is the first randomized controlled trial showing that a fully generative AI-based chatbot can reduce clinically significant symptoms. They also note that the result needs confirmation in larger samples.

Keep two points in mind when reading this finding. First, the study tested a bot developed specifically for this purpose, not general-purpose chatbots on the market. Second, the trial lasted four weeks and its control group consisted of people on a waitlist. The result is therefore a promising first step, but not enough to conclude that "chatbots work as therapy."

## Warnings from expert organizations

In November 2025, the APA issued a [health advisory](https://www.apa.org/topics/artificial-intelligence-machine-learning/health-advisory-chatbots-wellness-apps) on general-purpose generative AI chatbots and wellness apps. According to the advisory, these tools should not be used to provide psychotherapy or psychological treatment, because the evidence base is insufficient and regulation is lacking. The APA also highlights chatbots' tendency to validate users (sycophancy) as a risk, and it calls on policymakers to pass regulations that prohibit chatbots from posing as licensed professionals.

Another APA page lists groups that deserve particular caution: [people living with anxiety or obsessive-compulsive disorder, people prone to thought disorders, socially isolated people, adolescents and people who cannot access mental health services](https://www.apa.org/topics/artificial-intelligence-machine-learning/discussing-ai-use-therapy). If you have a child or teenager, our [AI guide for parents](/rehber/veliler-icin-yapay-zeka-rehberi/) may also be useful.

## Key risks

The risks identified in expert warnings and research can be summarized as follows:

- **Sycophancy:** Rather than questioning you, the bot may affirm you and reinforce unhealthy thoughts.
- **Limited coverage:** According to a Northeastern University report, researchers found that OpenAI's safeguards [do not cover many mental health conditions beyond suicide and self-harm](https://news.northeastern.edu/2026/07/27/chatgpt-lawsuit-ai-mental-health/). The same report says that when users conceal their intent, the safeguards are more likely to fail.
- **Lack of evidence:** According to the APA, the effectiveness of these tools for therapeutic purposes has not been sufficiently proven.
- **Privacy:** If you write very personal details, you need to know where and how the conversation is stored. For more on this topic, see the guide to [health data and AI privacy](/rehber/saglik-verisi-ve-yapay-zeka-gizlilik/).

## Legal restrictions in the United States

Illinois banned AI from providing therapy independently through the Wellness and Oversight for Psychological Resources Act (HB 1806). The state government's [press release](https://idfpr.illinois.gov/content/dam/soi/en/web/idfpr/news/2025/2025-08-04-idfpr-press-release-hb1806.pdf) announced that the law was signed in August 2025. The law prohibits offering and advertising therapy services except by licensed professionals. The Illinois Department of Financial and Professional Regulation (IDFPR) can impose administrative fines for violations, which can reach $10,000 per violation.

There is one area the law does not fully ban. [According to the Transparency Coalition](https://www.transparencycoalition.ai/news/illinois-gov-pritzer-signs-new-law-banning-unlicensed-ai-therapy-bots), AI may be used for administrative tasks such as scheduling, billing and general correspondence. The same source says Utah's law requires AI therapy bots to remind users that they are talking to a machine rather than a human, but it does not explicitly prohibit AI from providing therapy. These laws belong to U.S. states and do not apply in Türkiye.

## Safeguards from companies

In a [blog post](https://openai.com/index/update-on-mental-health-related-work/) dated February 27, 2026, OpenAI said parental controls were introduced in September 2025, that a feature allowing adult users to designate a "trusted contact" is coming soon, and that it has developed ways for ChatGPT to recognize signs of emotional distress.

In its [statement](https://openai.com/index/strengthening-chatgpt-responses-in-sensitive-conversations/) on the GPT-5 update, the company also reported that the rate of responses that violated its rules in mental health conversations fell by 65% in production traffic, and that undesired responses in expert evaluations fell by 39% compared with GPT-4o. These figures are the company's own measurements and do not amount to independent verification. According to the Northeastern report, multiple lawsuits against OpenAI have been consolidated into a single proceeding in California. In short, safeguards are ongoing, but their adequacy is still being debated.

## If you plan to use a chatbot

As a general framework, not medical guidance, consider the following:

1. **Treat the bot as a supportive tool.** It can be used for purposes such as journaling, organizing your thoughts or preparing what to tell a professional, but it should not be used for diagnosis or a treatment plan.
2. **Do not mistake agreement for validation.** If the bot keeps agreeing with you, that may be a warning sign. Ask a human for a different perspective.
3. **Share limited personal data.** Before writing identity details, addresses or detailed health information, check the privacy settings.
4. **Talk to a specialist.** If your symptoms are affecting your daily life, see a psychiatrist or psychologist. Sharing the bot you have been using with your therapist can also help.
5. **Add supervision for young people.** Talk openly with children or teenagers about their use of chatbots.

## What to do in a crisis

If you are thinking about harming yourself, or someone close to you is in immediate danger, do not chat with a bot. In Türkiye, call 112 in emergencies. If you have someone you trust nearby, talk to them and go to the nearest healthcare facility. It is not safe to assume a bot will always recognize a crisis: the research described above shows that safeguards do not cover every situation.

In summary, the evidence on AI chatbots for mental health is still at an early stage. There is a promising study for purpose-built tools, while expert organizations advise caution with general-purpose bots. To make decisions about your own situation, speak with a mental health professional.
