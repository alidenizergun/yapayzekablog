Generative AI has reopened debate over assignment, thesis and exam rules at universities. Students often do not know which uses are allowed and which are banned, while instructors struggle to prove who wrote a given text. This guide summarizes, in a practical framework, how these rules are taking shape, the limits of detection tools, changes to assignment and exam design, YÖK's ethics guide, and how to cite AI use. Institutional and course rules can differ; the binding policy is always your own university's and your course's written policy.

## Why university policies differ

Universities' AI policies do not follow a single template. Some institutions largely ban use, some allow it under specific conditions, and many leave the decision to the instructor for each course. [Thesify's roundup](https://www.thesify.ai/blog/generative-ai-policies-top-universities-2026) comparing approaches at leading universities worldwide is one example of this variety. Policy templates and implementation guides are also spreading; for instance, [Fabric's practical policy article for universities](https://fabric.so/blog/ai-policy-for-universities) describes how institutions can build a framework.

For students, the result is this: a use that one course permits may be banned in another in the same term. So rather than assuming a "general rule," check each course's guidelines. If the guidelines say nothing clear, ask your instructor in writing before using AI, and keep the reply.

## Why detection tools are not enough on their own

Tools that claim to identify AI-written text look appealing, but their reliability is disputed. [A study published on ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1472811723000605) examines, using ChatGPT as an example, the false positive (flagging human writing as AI) and false negative (missing AI text) errors of detection tools used in education and academic research. [Another study published in the International Journal for Educational Integrity](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40979-023-00146-z) focuses on testing AI-text detection tools. [Jisc's 2025 update](https://nationalcentreforai.jiscinvolve.org/wp/2025/06/24/ai-detection-assessment-2025/), from the UK organization that provides digital services to higher education, also covers detection and assessment.

The practical lesson from this work is that a detection score is a clue, not a verdict. A false alarm means a student who is wrongly under suspicion. A sound approach for institutions is to treat the score as a starting point and assess it alongside additional information, such as draft history, source use and a conversation with the student. For how these tools work and where their limits lie, you can also read the guide on [how to tell whether a text was written with AI](/rehber/yapay-zeka-ile-yazilmis-metin-nasil-anlasilir/).

If you are a student, you can take precautions against an unfair accusation: keep your drafts, notes and document version history. These records are the most concrete way to show that you produced the text yourself.

## Assignment and exam design is changing

The limits of detection tools are pushing many instructors to redesign assessment rather than try to catch misuse. [An arXiv study on AI-resistant assignments](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.23622) proposes a design that goes beyond detection, supporting critical thinking and offering automated feedback. The broader trend is to assess the process as well as the product.

In practice, this can take several forms:

- **Staged submission:** The outline, feedback and final text are submitted separately.
- **Process documentation:** Students are asked for a short note explaining their research notes or the tools they used.
- **Personalized topics:** Questions are tied to examples covered in class or to the student's own data.
- **Oral component:** The written work is supported by a short defense or a question-and-answer session. [An article in College Teaching](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/87567555.2025.2558563) discusses the concerns and logistics of oral exams in undergraduate humanities courses.

Each of these methods has a cost. Oral exams take time in large classes and can add strain for students who experience test anxiety. Rather than applying one method to every course, choosing the one that fits the course's goals is more balanced.

## What YÖK's ethics guide covers

In Turkey, the Council of Higher Education (YÖK), the top body overseeing higher education, has prepared a text titled "Ethics Guide on the Use of Generative Artificial Intelligence in Scientific Research and Publication Activities of Higher Education Institutions." As its title suggests, the guide focuses on the use of generative AI, particularly in scientific research and publication activities. For more on this, see [YÖK's own announcement](https://www.yok.edu.tr/tr/news/yuksekogretim-kurulu-yuksekogretim-kurumlari-bilimsel-arastirma-ve-yayin-faaliyetlerinde-uretken-yapay-zeka-kullanimina-dair-etik-rehber-hazirladi-zisSm) and [the Anadolu Agency's report](https://www.aa.com.tr/tr/egitim/yokten-uretken-yapay-zeka-kullanimina-dair-etik-rehber/3212359).

We recommend reading the guide yourself, because if you are preparing a thesis, article or conference paper, only the primary text definitively states which uses are considered appropriate. Summaries on third-party blogs may be incomplete or inaccurate. Confirm with your graduate institute or thesis advisor how the guide affects your university's own guidelines.

## How to cite AI use

Hiding AI use after relying on it is the biggest risk to academic integrity. Citation rules have not yet settled into a single standard; they vary by writing style, journal or course. University libraries are publishing guidance on this. For example, [the University of Alberta Libraries](https://guides.library.ualberta.ca/generative-ai/citation) and [Stony Brook University](https://guides.library.stonybrook.edu/genai/citing-ai) have published pages explaining how to disclose AI use.

A practical approach looks like this:

1. First, learn the rule of your course or journal; it may require its own format.
2. State the name of the tool you used and the purpose of its use (such as generating ideas, correcting language or summarizing).
3. If necessary, note the prompt you used and which part of the response you incorporated.
4. Verify information the AI produced. Models can generate fabricated information (hallucinations); a nonexistent source or incorrect citation is your academic responsibility.

Rather than presenting an AI tool as an author, most guidelines treat it as a tool that was used. Even so, the final word on this rests with your university's and the publisher's rules.

## A safe starting point for students

Using AI as a tool that supports learning rarely causes problems when it is used to understand concepts, review the language of a text you wrote yourself, or plan a study schedule. The problem begins when you hand over your thinking and writing effort and do not disclose it. For a broader framework, see the guide on [AI for students](/rehber/ogrenciler-icin-yapay-zeka/).

A short checklist:

- Have I read the course's AI rule?
- Can I state the tools I used and my purpose?
- Have I verified the information against another source?
- Am I keeping my drafts and notes?
- Have I avoided typing personal or confidential information into a chatbot? The guide on [protecting personal data](/rehber/yapay-zeka-kullanirken-kisisel-verileri-koruma/) will help here.

## Notes for instructors and institutions

Stating rules clearly, briefly and in writing at the start of a course prevents most later disputes. Explain permitted and prohibited uses with examples; a one-line ban such as "AI is prohibited" often does not explain what a student can and cannot do. When suspicion arises, talk with the student and request process records rather than deciding based on a detection score. For more on teachers, see the guide to AI for teachers and the [AI in education](/sektor/egitim/) page.

No single method solves the problem on its own. Academic integrity is better protected when clear policy, thoughtful assignment design, process-focused assessment and dialogue with students work together.
