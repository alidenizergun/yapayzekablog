In legal education, AI is now within easy reach for students studying, organizing notes and writing first drafts. The same tool can produce a nonexistent court decision with a highly convincing citation. This guide covers the uses, limits and verification habits that work for law students and trainee lawyers. It is not legal advice; in unclear situations, consult your professor, bar association or supervising lawyer.

## Where it helps and where it doesn't

Large language models (LLMs) can help with tasks such as summarizing text, explaining concepts and generating questions. For example, you can have one explain a difficult concept in three different ways, generate practice questions from the topic you are studying, or produce an outline of a subject from your own notes. In these uses, the goal is for AI to help you think, not to do the thinking for you.

That said, do not treat the output as a source. Because a model writes fluently, factual answers look correct even when they are not, and the risk of hallucination (fabricated information) is always present. For a detailed framework on this issue, see the [guide on the risk of fabricated case law in law](/rehber/hukukta-yapay-zeka-uydurma-ictihat-riski/).

A workflow that works in practice is this: first study the topic yourself and write your own answer, then use AI for comparison and to find what you missed. Writing a good prompt also improves the output quality; for basic techniques, see the [how to write a good prompt](/rehber/iyi-istem-nasil-yazilir/) guide.

## The role of AI in case law research

In case law research, AI can at most be a starting point. You can ask it which concepts to search under and which statute articles you should review. However, details such as the citation of a decision, date, chamber and case and decision numbers should never be taken directly from AI and placed in your text.

A Turkish case reported in the press illustrates this risk. According to the report, a Ankara lawyer with roughly 25 years of experience added Court of Cassation decisions to a petition with AI assistance, but the chamber, case and decision numbers did not appear in UYAP (Turkey's national judiciary informatics system) records. The court filed a criminal complaint, and the lawyer faced both a prosecutor's investigation and a bar investigation. The lawyer argued that the problem lay in the citation details, not in the content of the decisions ([Kısadalga](https://kisadalga.net/haber/ozel-haber/avukat-hakkinda-acilan-sorusturmanin-yapay-zekanin-ekledigi-hatali-ictihat-kunyelerinden-kaynaklandigi-one-suruldu-138719)). The process is ongoing, and it is not for us to reach a conclusion about this person. The lesson for us is that verifying citations is your responsibility.

A similar case occurred in Europe. The European Court of Human Rights (ECtHR) imposed a two-year ban on a Romanian lawyer's right to represent clients after his submissions cited nonexistent decisions and paragraphs that did not appear in real decisions. The lawyer acknowledged using AI and said he had not checked his sources sufficiently ([Haberler.com](https://www.haberler.com/guncel/aihm-baskani-yapay-zeka-kaynakli-ictihat-kullanan-20440785-haberi/)). Because sources give different dates for the ruling, we do not state a date here.

## Verification habit: step by step

A sound verification routine is the price of using AI. Apply these steps to every citation:

1. **Search for the decision in an official or reliable source.** If you cannot find the decision using the citation AI gave you, do not use the citation.
2. **Open and read the decision.** Do not rely on AI's summary or on claims like "the paragraph says..."; see the quotation and paragraph number in the source yourself.
3. **Check the citation details separately.** Confirm that the court, decision type, date and number are consistent with each other.
4. **Check the statute against the current text.** The article number and content may have changed.
5. **Do not write what you cannot find.** Do not leave in information you are unsure of with a note that it "probably exists."

The TBB's AI advisory guide for lawyers points the same way. It warns that AI may generate nonexistent case law, statutes or sources, and recommends that such output not be used without verification against official and reliable sources ([724 Güncel Haber](https://www.724guncelhaber.com/gundem/turkiye-barolar-birligi-avukatlar-icin-yapay-zeka-kullanimi-tavsiye-rehberi-yayinladi-109472)).

## The TBB guide for trainees

The Union of Turkish Bar Associations (TBB) published its "Guide to the Use of Artificial Intelligence for Lawyers" on August 28, 2026. The guide states that the lawyer is responsible for the final legal assessment and professional liability, and that AI output should not be submitted to courts, clients or third parties without review ([Zumbul Hukuk](https://www.zumbul.av.tr/tr/duyurular/turkiye-barolar-birligi-tarafindan-avukatlar-icin-yapay-zeka-kullanimi-tavsiye-rehberi-yayimlanmisti)).

The key point for trainees is that the guide covers trainee lawyers and office staff as well. It is described not as a binding regulation but as a professional standard that could serve as a benchmark for interpretation ([İMER Hukuk](https://imer.av.tr/rehber/yapay-zeka-hukuku-gelismeleri)). The guide classifies AI use into four groups: free auxiliary use, limited and controlled use, use subject to specific safeguards or approval, and prohibited use ([724 Güncel Haber](https://www.724guncelhaber.com/gundem/turkiye-barolar-birligi-avukatlar-icin-yapay-zeka-kullanimi-tavsiye-rehberi-yayinladi-109472)).

The guide also recommends that every firm create a written use policy suited to its size. The policy should specify which tools may be used, which data must not be entered into the system and how human review will be carried out ([Zumbul Hukuk](https://www.zumbul.av.tr/tr/duyurular/turkiye-barolar-birligi-tarafindan-avukatlar-icin-yapay-zeka-kullanimi-tavsiye-rehberi-yayimlanmisti)). If the firm where you are interning has such a policy, ask for it and read it first. Do not enter client information into a publicly available AI tool. For broader context, see the [AI for lawyers](/rehber/avukatlar-icin-yapay-zeka/) guide.

## Exam and assignment rules

Rules vary from school to school, so do not assume. Two examples from abroad show this variety. As of Summer 2026, Berkeley Law School banned all uses of AI in exams. For assignments, it banned use except in AI literacy courses where it is explicitly permitted, and it also prohibits students from uploading course materials to AI systems ([EdTech Innovation Hub](https://www.edtechinnovationhub.com/news/berkeley-law-sets-summer-2026-ai-policy-banning-use-in-exams-and-credited-coursework)).

In August 2026, Columbia Law replaced a strict temporary ban with a new policy. The policy treats AI as a "learning aid" for understanding study concepts, generating hypotheses and summarizing case law. It prohibits its use in submitted work and exams and requires students to disclose their use ([ABA Journal](https://www.abajournal.com/news/article/columbia-law-school-updates-ai-policy-with-new-restrictions)).

In Turkey, I found no binding general exam rule from the Council of Higher Education (YÖK) or from faculties. There is an example from academic publishing: the AI policy of the Erzincan Binali Yıldırım University Law Faculty Journal does not accept authors writing the introduction, main text or conclusion of an article with AI, and requires that citations generated by AI be checked against the original sources, namely articles, books and court decisions ([Dergipark](https://dergipark.org.tr/tr/pub/ebyuhfd/page/19482)). If you are unsure of the rule, ask your course instructor in writing and keep the reply. For academic integrity, see the [guide to AI and academic integrity at universities](/rehber/universitelerde-yapay-zeka-ve-akademik-durustluk/), and for Turkey-specific context, see the [AI in law in Turkey](/rehber/turkiyede-hukukta-yapay-zeka/) page.

## What students think

According to the Thomson Reuters Institute's 2026 Law Student Pulse Survey, 72% of US law students see AI literacy as an essential professional skill, while 74% worry that over-reliance will weaken core legal skills. About five in six students use AI for academic work several times a week. Forty-eight percent say policies vary by professor, and 32% say their school has not given them the AI skills they need for their careers ([Thomson Reuters Institute](https://www.thomsonreuters.com/en-us/posts/legal/law-student-pulse-survey-2026/)). These data are from the US and have no direct equivalent in Turkey, but they point to two concerns: skills and dependence.

## A safe approach to writing

In writing practice, use AI as an editor, not as an author. Write your own paragraph first, then ask for suggestions on grammar, flow and clarity. Do not present an argument you did not write as your own view; legal reasoning should be the product of your own thinking. When working on a case analysis or assignment, work out your own solution first, then compare it with the model's output and note the differences.

Before submitting a draft, use a short checklist: Did I open every source? Are the quotations word for word? Do I need to disclose my AI use? If the policy requires disclosure, state your use clearly, as in the Columbia example.

## Conclusion

AI can speed up legal work, but it does not remove the burden of verification. Verified sources, your own analysis and the written rules of your faculty or firm are the core habits that protect you when working with AI.
