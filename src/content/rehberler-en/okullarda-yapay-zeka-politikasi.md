Students are using AI tools for homework, and teachers are using them to prepare lessons, while school administrations face the question "How will we manage this?" Turkey has an official framework for this issue: the Ministry of National Education (MEB) has published a policy paper and an ethics guide. This guide outlines the main points of that framework, the YÖK guide for higher education, UNESCO's approach, and some country examples. It ends with a step-by-step explanation of how a school can write its own rules. For the details of official texts, you should always check the document itself.

## MEB's framework

According to the MEB [announcement](https://www.meb.gov.tr/egitimde-yapay-zeka-uygulamalari-etik-kilavuzu-yayimlandi/haber/39496/tr), the policy framework is based on the Artificial Intelligence in Education Policy Paper and Action Plan (2025-2029), which took effect on June 17, 2025. The Artificial Intelligence Applications Ethics Board Directive, based on this paper, took effect on October 22, 2025. The ministry's legislation file is cited as the legal basis for the directive, and information on this legislation is also available on the Board of Education and Discipline's [frequently asked questions page](https://ttkb.meb.gov.tr/www/sss.php).

In short, MEB's approach relies not on a single ban or a blanket declaration of freedom, but on a policy paper, an action plan, and a board structure that conducts ethical review. School-level rules are also expected to align with this framework.

## Who the guide covers

The scope of the ethics guide is broad. According to MEB's announcement, the guide covers all public schools of every type and level under the ministry, private education institutions, science and arts centers (BİLSEM), and public education centers. So if you work at a private school or teach at a BİLSEM, this framework is also a reference point for you.

If you want practical daily tips for teachers, see the [AI guide for teachers](https://www.meb.gov.tr/) and for the student side, see the [AI for students](/rehber/ogrenciler-icin-yapay-zeka/) guide.

## YÖK's guide for higher education

There is a separate document for universities. The Council of Higher Education (YÖK) prepared a guide titled [Ethical Guide on the Use of Generative AI in Scientific Research and Publication Activities of Higher Education Institutions](https://www.yok.edu.tr/tr/news/yuksekogretim-kurulu-yuksekogretim-kurumlari-bilimsel-arastirma-ve-yayin-faaliyetlerinde-uretken-yapay-zeka-kullanimina-dair-etik-rehber-hazirladi-zisSm). As the title suggests, its focus is scientific research and publication processes; it does not directly regulate primary and secondary schools.

If you work at the high school level, reading this guide can be useful as an example when writing rules on "academic integrity." For the university side, we have a guide on [AI and academic integrity at universities](/rehber/universitelerde-yapay-zeka-ve-akademik-durustluk/).

## UNESCO's approach

According to the United Nations news service, UNESCO called for [regulating the use of AI in schools](https://news.un.org/en/story/2023/09/1140477) in 2023. UNESCO also published a [guide](https://unesco.org.uk/site/assets/files/10375/guidance_for_generative_ai_in_education_and_research.pdf) on the use of generative AI in education and research. These documents put forward the idea that rules should be clearly set by governments and educational institutions.

When translating this framework to the scale of a school, one idea is useful: instead of banning the tool or leaving it unrestricted, write down the purpose for which it will be used, at what age, and under what oversight.

## An example from abroad: mandatory AI education in China

Some countries are moving forward through curriculum rather than regulation. According to a China Daily report, [Beijing has made AI education mandatory in public schools](https://www.chinadaily.com.cn/a/202510/21/WS68f6d9bea310f735438b601e.html). This example shows that AI can be treated not only as "a tool to be used" but also as a subject to be taught. Turkey's framework focuses on ethical use; the two approaches do not exclude each other.

When evaluating other countries' school policies, pay attention to the quality of the source: aggregator sites and blogs often lose detail when summarizing official documents. Whenever possible, go to the education ministry's own text of the country in question.

## Examples from Turkish schools and institutions

Alongside the official framework, institutions are publishing their own documents. The School Librarians Association has prepared an [AI policy for school libraries](https://www.okd.org.tr/images/pdf/OKD_YZ_Duyuru.pdf). Robert College's website also has a section titled [AI principles](https://website.robcol.k12.tr/tr/egitim-ogretim/yapay-zeka-ilkeleri/bu-belge-hakkinda). Do not copy these documents for your own school, but looking at their structure, the simplicity of their language, and the topics they cover is a good starting point.

## How to prepare your school's own rules

The steps below do not replace official documents; they are a draft working-process suggestion.

1. **Read the official framework first.** Do not write any rule that conflicts with the MEB ethics guide and policy paper. Ask your provincial or district National Education Directorate about points that remain unclear.
2. **Set up a working group.** It should include the principal, teachers from different subjects, the guidance counselor, the IT coordinator, and, if possible, a parent and student representative. If only management writes the rule, it will not get buy-in in practice.
3. **Map current use.** Which tools are students and teachers using, and for what? Start with a short survey. Rules written without knowing actual use will hang in the air.
4. **Write the purposes and limits.** Which activities allow free use (for example, idea generation or reviewing topics), which allow limited use (for example, submitting homework), and which are prohibited (for example, exams)? Make this distinction by subject and age group.
5. **Set a transparency rule.** If a student has received help from AI, how should they disclose it? A simple declaration sentence is often enough. Do not rely solely on AI-based text detection; the guide on [how to identify AI-written text](/rehber/yapay-zeka-ile-yazilmis-metin-nasil-anlasilir/) explains the limits of these methods.
6. **Protect personal data.** Make it a rule that student names, grades, health information, and similar data must not be entered into publicly available tools. For details, see the guide on [protecting personal data](/rehber/yapay-zeka-kullanirken-kisisel-verileri-koruma/).
7. **Provide training.** Give teachers and students a short introduction. Everyone needs to know that generative AI can produce made-up information (hallucinations) and can give biased outputs.
8. **Inform parents.** Parents should also see the rules; you can share resources such as the AI guide for parents for use at home.
9. **Date the policy and review it.** Tools change quickly. Write the version date of the policy and commit to reviewing it at regular intervals.

## Risks and limits

- **A total ban often does not work.** Students can already access the tools at home; a ban can make their use invisible. Still, clear bans are necessary in specific areas such as exams.
- **Inequality.** Not every student has the same access to devices and paid tools. Requiring AI for homework or expecting an advantage based on it may be unfair.
- **Accuracy.** Generative AI can present incorrect information with confidence. State clearly in your rule text that outputs must be verified against sources.
- **Excessive detail.** A list that counts every tool one by one becomes outdated quickly. Write principles and purposes of use rather than focusing on specific tools.
- **Enforcement.** Setting rules is not enough; it should also be clear what happens in case of a violation and how a student can defend themselves.

A school policy should be a short, understandable document that neither praises nor frightens about AI; it should clearly state who will do what, how, and with what responsibility. The most reliable method is to base it on the MEB framework and simplify it according to your school's real needs.
