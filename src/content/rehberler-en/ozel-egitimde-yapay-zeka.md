AI in special education and accessibility is not a single solution for every student but a family of assistive tools that reduce specific barriers at specific points. An app that describes a photo aloud for a visually impaired student, a tool that reads text aloud for a student with dyslexia, or a captioning system that turns classroom speech into text for a student who is deaf or hard of hearing are all parts of this family. This guide covers the types of tools, how to get started and where you need to be careful. If you are making decisions at the school level, the guide on [choosing AI tools for schools](/rehber/okullar-icin-yapay-zeka-araci-secimi/) will also help.

## Which tool type fits which need

Before looking for a tool, identify the barrier the student faces. The problem you want to solve should matter more than the product name.

- **Visual impairment:** Describing photos, screenshots, or printed or board materials; converting text to speech.
- **Dyslexia:** Listening to text read aloud, making long texts easier to understand, and support with spelling and reading.
- **Hearing impairment:** Converting speech to text in real time (live captions) and transcribing recorded lessons.
- **All groups:** Reworking course material to suit the student's level and needs.

Most of these tools rely on [generative AI](/sektor/egitim/) and natural language processing techniques. But the same technology does not produce the same result for every student, so testing and observation are essential.

## Students with visual impairments: image description

One of the best-known examples in image description is Be My AI, a feature within the Be My Eyes app. According to the company's support page, the user takes a photo, the tool describes the image, and the user can ask follow-up questions about that description. The tool is based on the [multimodal](/rehber/ozel-egitimde-yapay-zeka/) capabilities of OpenAI's GPT model ([Be My Eyes: Be My AI](https://support.bemyeyes.com/hc/en-us/articles/17493302011921-Be-My-AI-Image-to-text-assistance)).

For a student, the practical use could be asking about a diagram in a textbook, a note on the board, or a label and getting an answer. Being able to ask follow-up questions makes the use more flexible than a one-time description; the student can ask again about a detail that was missing.

## How to get started with Be My AI

According to the support page, Be My AI became available to all iOS, Android and Windows desktop users with a July 1, 2026 update. It is said to be compatible with the VoiceOver and TalkBack screen readers ([Be My Eyes: Using Be My AI](https://support.bemyeyes.com/hc/en-us/articles/18133134809105-Using-Be-My-AI)). To get started, you can follow these steps:

1. Install the app on the device the student uses, and try it together with the screen reader to confirm they work together.
2. Start with low-risk images first, such as an object in the classroom or a worksheet.
3. Teach the student how to ask good questions: telling the tool what to focus on can be more useful than simply asking for a description. The principles in the guide on [writing good prompts](/rehber/iyi-istem-nasil-yazilir/) apply here too.
4. Check Turkish support. The Google Play page says the AI feature works in 36 languages, but the page does not make clear whether Turkish is on that list ([Google Play: Be My Eyes](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=com.bemyeyes.bemyeyes&hl=en_US)). Try the app with Turkish questions and evaluate the results yourself.

According to the same support page, Be My AI is not supported on wearable devices. The company states that keeping the tool free is part of its business plan.

## Other options for students with visual impairments

The Turkish Ministry of National Education (MEB) handbook on AI for teachers lists apps such as Seeing AI, Google Lookout and Braigo for students with visual impairments. According to the handbook, Seeing AI can identify objects, text and facial expressions. The same handbook includes an example in which a set of lecture notes was converted into an audiobook using ElevenLabs ([MEB: AI teacher handbook](https://yegitek.meb.gov.tr/meb_iys_dosyalar/2026_04/69d3c65226ce6871904789_egitim_yapayzeka_ogretmen_elkitabi.pdf)). This example shows that the same content can be converted into different formats, but the teacher must check the quality and accuracy of the audiobook.

## Dyslexia: reading and writing support

For students with dyslexia, AI tools can usually help in three ways: reading text aloud, restating long and dense text in simpler language, and showing spelling errors while writing. All of these aim to reduce the student's reading load, not to replace learning.

A practical approach is for the student to try reading on their own first and use audio support only where they struggle. In this way, the tool acts like a crutch and does not take over the development of reading skills. When using a simplified text, make it a habit to compare it with the original, because AI may shift the meaning while simplifying or may skip an important detail.

Be skeptical of product claims in this area. Instead of marketing copy that says a tool helps with dyslexia, look for independent, peer-reviewed studies; a short trial with your own student is often the most reliable evidence.

## Students who are deaf or hard of hearing: live captions

For students who are deaf or hard of hearing, tools that convert speech into text in real time can make participation in class easier. When setting up, consider the following:

- **Audio quality:** The teacher being close to the microphone and low classroom noise both affect accuracy.
- **Proper nouns and terms:** Course terminology, foreign words and proper names may be transcribed incorrectly. Share key terms with the student before the lesson begins.
- **Multiple speakers:** In discussions and group work, it can be hard to tell who is speaking.
- **Turkish performance:** Test the tool with a real lesson excerpt; a system that works well in English may not perform equally well in Turkish.

Automatic captions may not replace a sign language interpreter or a written note-taker for some students. The student, family and relevant specialist should decide together which support is sufficient.

## Preparing individualized materials

One of the areas where AI holds the most promise in special education is material adaptation: writing the same topic in shorter sentences, adding examples, adjusting the number of exercises, or converting content to audio. It can save teachers time, but you need to read the output before giving it to the student. AI can produce [made-up information (hallucinations)](/rehber/yapay-zeka-ile-kisisellestirilmis-ogrenme/); an incorrect definition or a flawed example creates a bigger problem for a student who already struggles to access information.

Material that does not match the student's individualized education plan or skips its goals is useless, no matter how polished it looks. Check the output against the plan.

## Limits and risks

**Privacy.** According to the support page for Be My AI, photos and chats are sent to OpenAI for processing, and chat history is archived for 30 days ([Be My Eyes: Using Be My AI](https://support.bemyeyes.com/hc/en-us/articles/18133134809105-Using-Be-My-AI)). Students, classmates and families should think about this before sharing photos that show identifiable people or private information. For broader principles, see the guide on [protecting personal data when using AI](/rehber/yapay-zeka-kullanirken-kisisel-verileri-koruma/).

**Accuracy.** Description and captioning tools can make mistakes. In situations with serious consequences, such as a medication label, an exam question or a safety warning, do not rely on a single tool.

**Dependency.** Using the tool should not prevent the student from developing their own skills. The goal is to make the student more independent.

**Inequality.** Not every student may have a suitable device, internet connection or support. For a school-level policy, you can review the guide on [AI policy in schools](/rehber/okullarda-yapay-zeka-politikasi/).

## Checklist before you start

- Define the student's need and goal in writing.
- Test the tool first with a short, low-risk trial.
- Read the privacy and data terms; share them with school administration and the family if needed.
- Test Turkish performance with real content.
- Explain to the student and family when they can trust the tool and when they should verify the output.
- Review the process regularly, and do not hesitate to drop a tool that is not working.

AI-powered tools can make access easier, but the best results come when the teacher, family and specialist who understand the student's needs guide the tool together. For individual cases, consult the Guidance and Research Center (RAM) or the relevant specialist.
