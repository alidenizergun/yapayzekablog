A loan application may no longer be decided solely by an analyst or a fixed scorecard. Banks and fintech companies have begun using machine learning models to predict credit risk. This guide explains roughly how these systems work, the EU and Turkish frameworks, and what you can do if you are rejected. It is not legal advice; consult an expert for a specific dispute.

## How does AI work in credit scoring?

Traditional scoring looks at your past payment behavior and debt position. Machine learning-based models try to predict the probability of default (non-payment) by weighing many variables together. The output is usually a score or an approve/deny recommendation. Whether the model or a human makes the final decision varies by institution, and it is not visible from the outside.

This approach has two sides. When built correctly, it can catch patterns that traditional methods miss. However, the model may carry biases from its training data and may struggle to explain its reasoning. For the banking side of the topic, you may also want to read our [guide to AI in banking](/rehber/bankacilikta-yapay-zeka/).

## Alternative data: opportunities and limits

Alternative data refers to information outside classic credit history. According to a [BloombergHT column](https://www.bloomberght.com/yorum/koray-gultekin-bahar/3775253-kredi-skoru-gecmisin-notu-degil-gelecegin-tahmini-olmali), this data can range from mobile payment habits and bill payment patterns to e-commerce behavior and income flows. The same column says academic studies show these models can achieve higher accuracy than traditional methods in some segments, but it does not identify which studies these are.

The expected benefit is that people without a credit history can also be assessed. This claim appears more often in promotional content, so independent verification is needed. [Yapay Zeka Derneği's (Turkey's AI association) article on financial inclusion](https://ai.org.tr/finansal-kapsayicilik-nedir-yapay-zeka-nasil-kullanilabilir/) lists phone usage data and social media behavior among alternative data examples, but it does not address how this data should be handled under privacy rules or the KVKK. This is an important open question in practice: you may want to know which of your data is used in scoring.

## Denial reasons and explainability: the US example

Explainability means a model's decision can be clearly explained. The clearest examples on this come from the US. In a guidance document, the [US Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB)](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/archive/newsroom/cfpb-issues-guidance-on-credit-denials-by-lenders-using-artificial-intelligence/) stated that lenders cannot use sample denial forms and checklists if they do not reflect the actual reason for denial. If a credit limit is reduced based on behavioral spending data, the explanation should not stop at a general phrase such as "shopping history"; it should describe the specific negative behavior.

There is a current development: according to reports, the CFPB circulars on this topic were withdrawn on May 12, 2025. According to an [analysis by MightyBot](https://mightybot.ai/blog/adverse-action-notices-ai-credit-decisions/), the withdrawal is not definitive, and the legal requirements (ECOA and Regulation B) remain in force. In other words, the principle in the US has not changed; only the status of the guidance is disputed. This example does not directly apply in Turkey, but it shows where the idea that "using AI does not exempt a lender from giving reasons" comes from.

## High-risk classification under the EU AI Act

The EU AI Act lists credit scoring among the systems it treats as high-risk. [RegulatoryAI](https://www.regulatoryai.eu/ai-financial-services/) states that AI used to evaluate the creditworthiness of natural persons or to produce credit scores is classified as high-risk under Annex III, point 5(b). The details of this classification are as follows:

- The classification depends on purpose, not the data source. According to [DeepInspect](https://www.deepinspect.ai/blog/eu-ai-act-for-credit-scoring), using alternative data does not change the scope.
- The scope covers natural persons; the creditworthiness of companies does not fall within this category.
- According to [ComplyLayer](https://complylayer.com/use-cases/ai-credit-scoring), institutions using the system for credit decisions must conduct a fundamental rights impact assessment (FRIA, Article 27) before first use.

Sources conflict on the effective date. One source says the high-risk requirements take effect on August 2, 2026, while DeepInspect states that the obligations have been postponed to December 2, 2027. Because the official EU text could not be verified for this guide, you should check the current date in the EU's official publications. For the impact of the law on Turkey, see our [guide to the EU AI Act and Turkey](/rehber/ab-yapay-zeka-yasasi-ve-turkiye/).

## Credit Registry Bureau (KKB) and the loan assessment process in Turkey

At the center of credit assessment in Turkey are the Credit Registry Bureau (KKB) and its Findeks products. According to [KKB's 2025 sustainability report](https://www.kkb.com.tr/Resources/ContentFile/4135/2025surdurulebilirlikraporu.pdf), Findeks products allow financial institutions to assess using more current, accurate and comprehensive data. The same report describes the "First Payment Score" under the Agricultural Loans Assessment System (TARDES): a score that measures the risk of non-payment from the first installment on consumer loan applications.

The report also lists artificial intelligence among the product categories under "Kloudeks," but it provides no details on how this is used in credit scoring. Therefore, this source cannot tell us how much of KKB's scores are produced with machine learning. It is also difficult to say anything definitive about an AI-specific credit regulation in Turkey; the existing framework is mainly read through personal data law. For regulatory developments in the sector, our [guide to AI regulations in the financial sector](/rehber/finans-sektorunde-yapay-zeka-duzenlemeleri/) is a good starting point.

## Your rights under the KVKK

According to the [KVKK's page on data subject rights](https://www.kvkk.gov.tr/Icerik/2036/Ilgili-Kisinin-Haklari), under Article 11 you have the right to object when data processed is analyzed exclusively by automated systems and results in an adverse outcome for you. If your loan application was rejected solely by an automated system, this right may come into play.

The scope is shaped by legal interpretation. For this reason, how far the right to object extends, for example whether you can request that the decision be re-evaluated, may be debated. To understand your personal data rights in general, our [guide to KVKK and AI tools](/rehber/kvkk-ve-yapay-zeka-araclari/) and [guide to personalization and KVKK](/rehber/yapay-zeka-ile-kisisellestirme-ve-kvkk/) may help.

Sources also disagree on whether giving denial reasons is legally mandatory. [A credit advisory blog](https://www.saglamkredim.com/blog/kredi-basvurusu-reddedildi-ne-yapmaliyim-2026) says banks are not required to explain denial reasons in detail, while other sources argue the opposite. Given this uncertainty, it is more practical to ask the bank in writing rather than assume you are entitled to a reason.

## Practical steps if you are rejected

The steps below are not legal advice; they are a general roadmap:

1. **Apply to the bank in writing.** Ask for the reason your application was rejected and whether the decision was made solely by an automated system.
2. **Use your KVKK right.** If the decision was based solely on automated analysis, state your right to object under Article 11 in writing.
3. **Keep the response.** Keep records of correspondence and the bank's reply; they will be useful for future applications.
4. **Consult an expert.** One law firm notes that decisions under the KVKK can be challenged before the administrative court (Article 18), but this should be checked against the official text. For significant disputes, get support from a lawyer.

## Limits to keep in mind

In this area, claims often move faster than real evidence. The claim that alternative data produces fairer results mostly comes from promotional content. In Turkey, sources disagree on whether giving denial reasons is mandatory. The timeline for application in the EU is also uncertain. So when exercising your rights, consult the current texts of official institutions, and read blog and advisory articles only as background information.
