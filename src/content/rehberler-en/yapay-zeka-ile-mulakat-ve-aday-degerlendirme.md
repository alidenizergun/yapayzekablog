AI-powered interview and candidate evaluation tools are being sold to HR teams facing heavy application volumes, with the promise of saving time. But not all of these tools carry the same level of risk. Transcribing an interview and reading a candidate's “emotional state” from their face and voice are very different things. This guide explains which uses are reasonable, which are scientifically and legally problematic, and how you can make your process fairer. This guide is for general information only and is not legal advice.

## Where AI support can be reasonable

It helps to split AI in hiring into two groups. The first group is text- and content-based work that focuses on what the candidate says: transcribing interview recordings, summarizing notes, preparing a standard set of questions, and organizing answers according to criteria you have set in advance. In these tasks the tool is only an aid, and a human can review its output. For recordings of meetings, see our [meeting notes and transcription guide](/rehber/yapay-zeka-ile-toplanti-notu-ve-transkript/).

The second group consists of tools that process a candidate's face, voice or body language to draw conclusions about personality, confidence or emotional state. The real problems begin in this group. Most of the sections below concern it.

## Video interview analysis and automated screening

In automated screening, a candidate answers pre-recorded questions on video or audio. The system records the answers, transcribes them and, in some products, scores them. This setup requires you to make two separate decisions. First, will the system assess only the content of the answer, or will it also process visual and audio features? Second, will the score decide the outcome, or will it only be an input for a human reviewer?

As a practical principle, keep the focus on content, ask every candidate the same questions under the same conditions, and write your scoring criteria in advance. When evaluating a product, ask the vendor which data the system processes and how the score is produced. Eliminating a candidate with a system that cannot explain its output creates a position that is hard to defend when challenged.

## EU AI Act: the emotion recognition ban

The EU AI Act prohibits placing on the market and using AI systems that infer emotions in the workplace. The only exceptions apply to medical or safety uses. The law's rationale notes that the reliability of emotion recognition systems is widely recognized as weak ([CDT](https://cdt.org/insights/eu-ai-act-brief-pt-4-ai-at-work/)). The ban appears in Article 5(1)(f) of the law.

The scope is the critical point for HR. The European Commission's guidelines read the concept of “workplace” broadly, and hiring and probationary periods fall within it ([Artificial Intelligence Act](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/prohibited-ai-practices-under-the-ai-act/)). According to a legal blog's commentary, any system that evaluates a candidate's emotions during an interview falls under the ban because there is no clear exception ([Technology's Legal Edge](https://www.technologyslegaledge.com/2025/04/eu-ai-act-spotlight-on-emotional-recognition-systems-in-the-workplace/)). The ban has applied since February 2, 2025; this date appears consistently in secondary sources ([Confir](https://confir.eu/annex-iii/emotion-recognition-workplace)).

It is also important to know the limits of the ban. According to the guidelines, the ban covers emotions derived from biometric data such as faces and voices. Sentiment analysis of written text, and detection of physical states such as pain or fatigue, fall outside it. The same source notes that inferring the emotions of call center employees may be prohibited, while the same kind of system evaluating customers' emotions does not fall under Article 5(1)(f) ([Artificial Intelligence Act](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/prohibited-ai-practices-under-the-ai-act/)). When applying the law and guidelines to your own process, verify the details with your legal adviser. For the broader framework, see our [EU AI Act and Turkey guide](/rehber/ab-yapay-zeka-yasasi-ve-turkiye/).

## Scientific critiques of face and voice analysis

The ban is not based solely on legal considerations; there is also a scientific debate. A 2019 review by Lisa Feldman Barrett and her team examined more than 1,000 studies in the psychology literature. It concluded that there is no scientific basis for the assumption that a person's emotional state can be reliably read from facial movements ([ACLU](https://www.aclu.org/news/privacy-technology/experts-say-emotion-recognition-lacks-scientific)). We are relaying this from the ACLU's summary; we have not seen the original paper. According to the review, it is not possible to confidently infer happiness from a smile or anger from furrowed brows.

A peer-reviewed study from 2022 that examines the technical reliability of facial emotion recognition systems reaches a similar conclusion: the authors state that these systems cannot be said to have real accuracy for practical purposes ([Big Data & Society](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/20539517221129549)). Moreover, in an interview setting, a candidate may be nervous, may come from a different cultural background, and camera and lighting conditions may vary. Any of these can be misread as “insecurity” or “reluctance.”

For this reason, if a vendor offers a “confidence score” or “sentiment analysis,” treat it as a contested prediction, not as a scientifically proven measurement.

## Examples from the US: notice, consent and audits

Outside the EU, some US states take a different approach: instead of banning, they require transparency and auditing. According to a compliance guide, Illinois's Artificial Intelligence Video Interview Act has been in force since 2020. It requires employers to notify candidates before an interview that their video will be analyzed using AI, to explain which characteristics the system evaluates, and to obtain written consent ([AI Laws by State](https://www.ailawsbystate.com/blog/ai-hiring-laws-state-guide-2026)). New York City's Local Law 144 requires an annual independent bias audit for automated hiring tools and notice to candidates. We are relaying this from a hiring software blog and have not verified the law itself ([NinjaHire](https://ninjahire.co/thoughts/ai-hiring-laws-by-state)).

These examples do not apply in Turkey, but they show what good practice looks like: tell candidates, explain what is being measured, and independently test the tool's results. We cover the issue of bias separately in our [AI in hiring and bias guide](/rehber/yapay-zeka-ile-ise-alim-ve-onyargi/).

## When candidates use AI

The roles on both sides are changing: candidates may use AI to prepare CVs, cover letters and interview answers. The healthiest approach here is to base your response on rules rather than on guesses. Clearly state in the job posting or invitation message which stages allow AI assistance and which limit it. Do not judge answers by whether they seem memorized or generated; compare them using the same live questions and work samples. The live, interactive part of an interview is the most direct way to see a candidate's knowledge. For candidates preparing for interviews, we have a [guide to preparing for interviews with AI](/rehber/yapay-zeka-ile-mulakata-hazirlanma/).

## Checklist for a fair process

- **Define the purpose:** Document the decision the tool is used for and what it does not measure.
- **Avoid face, voice and emotion analysis:** If you operate in a process connected to the EU, keep these features turned off or do not use them, and consult your legal adviser.
- **Inform candidates:** State at the outset where AI is used, what data is processed and how long the data will be kept.
- **Keep a human decision:** Do not leave elimination to a single score; provide a path for appeal and reconsideration.
- **Audit:** Regularly check how the tool performs across different candidate groups and ask the vendor for explanations.
- **Limit data:** Do not keep interview recordings longer than necessary. For obligations related to personal data, see our [KVKK (Turkey's Personal Data Protection Law) and AI tools guide](/rehber/kvkk-ve-yapay-zeka-araclari/).

## What to do in Turkey

While preparing this guide, we did not find a Turkish text that directly regulates AI-powered interviews. In practice, general rules such as personal data protection and the prohibition of discrimination, along with your company's own internal policies, will be decisive. Before adopting a new tool, evaluate it together with your legal and information security teams. If you have a specific product in hand, ask the vendor for its data flows in writing.
