With AI assistants now widespread, teachers, editors and employers are asking the same question: Did a human write this text, or an AI? The short answer: there is no reliable way to know for certain. But there are some clues and good practices.

## How do detection tools work?

AI text detectors generally measure how "predictable" a text is. Because language models choose likely words at each step, the text they produce can be statistically more regular. The tools measure this regularity and sentence variety, then output a probability.

## Why aren't they reliable?

- **False alarms:** Plain, rule-following human writing with formulaic phrasing, especially texts by writers whose native language is different, can be flagged as AI.
- **Easy to evade:** When AI-written text is lightly edited or paraphrased with another tool, the tools' accuracy drops markedly.
- **Models keep changing:** As newer models write more naturally, older detection methods become obsolete.
- **Turkish:** Most tools are developed mainly with English text, so they may be less reliable on Turkish text.

## Clues worth noticing

The signs below are not proof on their own, but they can stand out when seen together:

- A general, superficial treatment of the topic; a lack of personal experience, concrete examples and original opinion.
- Nonexistent sources, fabricated quotes or incorrect dates.
- A style and word choice noticeably different from the person's earlier writing.
- Inconsistency with details discussed in class or in a meeting.
- An overly balanced structure that presents everything as "on one hand, on the other hand" and never reaches a clear conclusion.

## Recommendations for educators

1. **Set the rule up front:** State clearly which assignments allow AI and which prohibit it.
2. **Evaluate the process:** Asking for drafts, notes and version history is healthier than looking only at the final product.
3. **Oral defense:** Asking students to explain their assignment by answering a few questions is one of the most reliable methods.
4. **Redesign the assignment:** Assignments that require personal experience, in-class work or local topics cannot be easily done with AI.
5. **Don't accuse based on a tool's result:** A high score from a detector can be a reason to talk with the student, but it should not be the sole basis for a penalty.

## For readers and editors

For news, evaluations or social media content, the key question should be not "who wrote it?" but "is it true?" Verifying information at its source is the most reliable approach, regardless of whether the content was produced with AI.
