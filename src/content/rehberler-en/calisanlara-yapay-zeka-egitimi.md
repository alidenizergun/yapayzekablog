Many companies have put AI tools in the hands of their employees, but how much they have taught people to use them is debatable. Regulators and workplace data both point in the same direction: distributing tools is not enough on its own. Teams need to know what they should use AI for, how to use it and within which limits. This guide summarizes how AI literacy programs can be set up, the approaches of the EU and Turkey, free resources and ways to measure the impact of training.

## What is AI literacy and why does it matter?

AI literacy means that an employee knows what the tool they use can do, where it can make mistakes and which data should not be entered into it. Because generative AI tools can produce fabricated information (hallucinations), knowing how to check output is at the core of this literacy. Writing a good prompt is also part of the job; for guidance, see our [how to write a good prompt](/rehber/iyi-istem-nasil-yazilir/) guide.

The key point here is that literacy is not achieved in a single seminar. The same tool carries different risks for an HR specialist and for a software developer.

## What does Article 4 of the EU AI Act say?

Article 4 asks organizations that provide and use AI systems to take steps to ensure AI literacy among their staff and among others who operate AI on their behalf. According to [Travers Smith's analysis](https://www.traverssmith.com/knowledge/knowledge-container/the-eu-ai-acts-ai-literacy-requirement-key-considerations/), the obligation has applied since February 2, 2025.

The 2026 Digital Omnibus amendment softened the wording. Instead of “ensuring a sufficient level of AI literacy,” the text now speaks of “taking measures to support the development of AI literacy,” and it states that literacy at the individual level does not have to be guaranteed. As [Token Academy reported](https://tokenacademy.ai/blog/eu-ai-act-article-4-ai-literacy), the change took effect in 2026. Secondary sources give different dates for the exact publication and entry-into-force day, so it is worth verifying the date against the official text. A similar caution applies to the audit timeline and possible penalties: commentary on these topics mostly relies on consultancy articles.

[An article published on Dergipark](https://dergipark.org.tr/tr/download/article-file/5852696) considers a compliance approach that reduces the article to the question “Was training delivered?” inadequate. According to the author, organizations should set literacy levels based on their risk situations and record them. For broader context, see our [EU AI Act and Turkey](/rehber/ab-yapay-zeka-yasasi-ve-turkiye/) guide.

## Mandatory training planned for the Turkish public sector

According to reports dated September 2, 2026, basic AI literacy training will be delivered via e-learning to public-sector employees under the 2026-2030 Türkiye AI Action Plan, coordinated by the Ministry of Industry and Technology. As [reported by İktibas Dergisi](https://iktibasdergisi.com/2026/09/02/kamu-personeline-zorunlu-yapay-zeka-egitimi/), the training will be made mandatory through the human resources systems of public institutions, and short job-specific practical modules will be added. An AI manager will be appointed in each institution, and institutions will prepare a three-year roadmap within 12 weeks.

The plan offers a concrete example of how an organization can structure literacy: central basic training, job-based modules on top, and a responsible person within the organization. However, the scope and timeline of the training depend on implementation rules to be announced by the relevant institutions, so the details may change. The sources do not mention a similar obligation for the private sector.

## Workplace data: a large training gap

The gap between access to tools and training is also reflected in surveys. According to [FirstMovers](https://firstmovers.ai/ai-training-for-employees/), a Docebo survey conducted in April 2026 with 2,000 employees and learning managers across six countries found that 85% of employees say the training they received did not help them use AI in their roles. One in five respondents said they had received no training at all. The same source also cites a March 2026 report by Jobs for the Future: 36% of workers believe they have the training and resources needed to use AI, down from 45% the previous year.

The share of employees receiving training from their employer also varies by workforce group. [A compilation based on the same data](https://aiservicescompanies.com/research/employee-ai-training-statistics-2026/) gives 42% for white-collar workers and 28% for blue-collar workers. Because these figures come from blogs and compilation sites, they should not be treated as definitive without seeing the original reports. Even so, they suggest that unequal access to training is a issue worth taking seriously.

## How do you set up a training program?

The common idea in the sources is to build a layered structure rather than a one-size-fits-all presentation. You can start with these steps:

- **Map current use.** Which tools are your employees using, and for which tasks? Look at approved and unapproved use together.
- **Provide a common foundation first.** What AI does, where it makes errors and which data must not be shared should be the same for everyone. Consider internal rules alongside our [company AI policy](/rehber/sirket-ici-yapay-zeka-politikasi/) guide.
- **Add role-specific practice.** HR, finance, marketing and software teams should practice with their real work. The job-based module logic in the public-sector plan supports this approach.
- **Make personal data a separate topic.** One of the most common risks is employees pasting customer or candidate information into prompts. Our [personal data protection guide](/rehber/yapay-zeka-kullanirken-kisisel-verileri-koruma/) can be used for this section.
- **Assign someone responsible.** The idea of an AI manager from the public-sector example can be applied in the private sector as a coordinator or a small working group.

For individual learning, the article [where to start learning AI](/rehber/yapay-zeka-ogrenmeye-nereden-baslamali/) is another resource you can recommend to your employees.

## Free training resources

If you do not have a budget to start, free courses can help. A [list shared on X](https://x.com/nrqa__/status/2039360809839808835) outlines free AI courses for 2026 from Anthropic, Google, Meta, Nvidia, Microsoft, OpenAI and IBM. The source is one person's compilation, so check each course's content, currency and certificate status on its own page. These courses teach general concepts. You will need to add your own content for your company's toolset, data rules and workflows.

## How do you measure the impact of training?

Participation and completion rates are easy to measure but not sufficient. Keeping records is a reasonable approach for Article 4; the real question, however, is whether the employee is doing their job better and more safely. Short role-based assessments, compliance with usage rules, how often faulty output is caught and teams' own feedback can all be used for this purpose.

A piece published in the California Management Review at Berkeley argues that [training increases adoption but does not by itself deliver sustainable productivity](https://cmr.berkeley.edu/2026/10/why-ai-training-is-driving-adoption-but-not-sustainable-productivity/). In other words, even if tool usage rises, you may not see lasting improvement in the quality of work output. Comparing the time and quality of the same tasks before and after training is a practical way to see this gap.

## Risks and limits

- If training becomes a one-time event, it will be forgotten. Content should be updated as tools and rules change.
- Treating Article 4 only as a documentation exercise misses its real purpose.
- Sources interpret the scope of legal obligations differently. Rely on the official text and your legal adviser. For risks in HR processes, it is also worth looking at [AI in hiring and bias](/rehber/yapay-zeka-ile-ise-alim-ve-onyargi/).

In short, an effective program is small but practical, tailored to roles and judged by behavior.
