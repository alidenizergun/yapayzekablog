Reports that children turn to AI chatbots for everything from homework to companionship are now common. In a US survey, [according to NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/10/28/nx-s1-6016285/most-tweens-and-teens-use-ai-for-schoolwork-and-emotional-support-a-new-survey-shows), a study by the Jed Foundation of more than 5,000 young people found that 58% said they used chatbots for schoolwork. There is no comparable research figure for Turkey, but the tools are equally accessible there. This guide aims to help parents practically address age limits, supervision settings, the limits on homework help and emotional risks. For the general framework, you can also read our [guide to AI for parents](/rehber/veliler-icin-yapay-zeka-rehberi/).

## Age limits: what we know and what we don't

According to OpenAI's [help page](https://help.openai.com/en/articles/8313401), ChatGPT is not a product designed for children; parental permission is required for users aged 13-18. So a child under 13 using their own account is not a use the company intends.

OpenAI uses a service called Persona for age verification. According to the [ChatGPT for Teens page](https://help.openai.com/en/articles/20001421-chatgpt-for-teens), teen protections are removed from accounts verified as 18 or older. That is why it matters to check the age the account was opened with.

Age requirements for other tools, such as Gemini and Claude, can vary by country and product. Sources give different ages on this point, so for accurate information you should read the current terms of use of the company in question yourself. Checking this page before recommending a tool to your child is safer than guessing.

## How ChatGPT parental controls work

The system is built on linking accounts. According to [OpenAI's announcement](https://openai.com/index/introducing-parental-controls/), you link your own account to your child's account, and the child must accept the invitation to connect. So the process cannot be carried out without your child knowing.

Once the link is established, the settings you can adjust include:

- Quiet hours (turning off access during set times)
- Sensitive content filters
- Turning off the use of conversations for model training
- Turning on Study Mode for your child's account

These options are described in [OpenAI's help page](https://help.openai.com/en/articles/12315553-managing-parental-controls-in-chatgpt) and the announcement post. While linked, the child cannot change these settings on their own. Either you or your child can remove the link at any time. Parental controls also end when the child turns 18 or when the account is recognized as an adult account.

Where to start? First create an account yourself and try the tool, then look at the settings together with your child. For an overview of the tool, our [ChatGPT page](/arac/chatgpt/) and [how to use ChatGPT guide](/rehber/chatgpt-nasil-kullanilir/) may be useful.

## The limits of supervision

Knowing what parental controls do not do is as important as knowing what they do. According to OpenAI, the link does not let you see your child's chats, chat history or real-time activity. So this tool is not surveillance; it is a settings panel.

[Common Sense Media's parents' guide](https://www.commonsensemedia.org/articles/parents-ultimate-guide-to-chatgpt-parental-controls) raises two warnings. First, safety notifications may not always be reliable in emergencies. Second, children can create a separate, unlinked account. For this reason, the organization recommends that parental controls be considered together with ongoing attention and family rules. A technical setting does not replace conversation.

## Homework help versus cheating

The MEB (Turkey's Ministry of National Education) [Recommendations on AI Ethics](https://oybo.meb.k12.tr/meb_iys_dosyalar/27/06/747178/dosyalar/2026_04/69e5e7b9db4b2949003351_YAPAY_ZEKA_ET%C4%B0%C4%9E%C4%B0_TAVS%C4%B0YELER%C4%B0.pdf) includes, among its recommendations for students, not accepting AI suggestions immediately and not becoming overly dependent on it. The section for parents recommends monitoring the child's AI use regularly without going to extremes, and providing information appropriate to the child's age.

You can use a practical test to draw the line on cheating: can your child explain the result, and can they rewrite it in their own words? A ready-made answer handed in without understanding is cheating; asking for a topic to be explained, having sample questions solved, or asking it to point out errors in the text they wrote is support. Concrete examples:

- Acceptable: Having a topic they don't understand explained in a different way, asking for ideas on their own draft, having the logic of a solved problem checked.
- Problematic: Pasting the homework question and submitting the answer as is, presenting text written by AI as their own work.

The final word belongs to the teacher. Since rules can vary from assignment to assignment, ask your child to check with their teacher about which assignments allow AI and how it may be used. Chatbots can produce made-up information (hallucinations), so comparing answers with the textbook is also a good habit. For the student side, see our [guide to AI for students](/rehber/ogrenciler-icin-yapay-zeka/), and for the school side, our [article on school AI policies](/rehber/okullarda-yapay-zeka-politikasi/).

## Emotional bonding risks with chatbots

The problem is not only homework. In the survey reported by NPR, a similar proportion of young people turn to chatbots when they feel sad, stressed or lonely. Common Sense Media's [research](https://institute.commonsensemedia.org/risk-assessments/social-ai-companions?gate=riskassessment) on 1,060 US teens aged 13-17 highlights these figures:

- 72% of teens have tried AI companions at least once.
- 52% use them regularly, and 13% use them every day.
- One in three teens chose to talk about an important matter with an AI companion instead of a real person.
- 31% find these conversations as satisfying as those with real friends.

To keep this balanced, it should be added that in the same research, 80% of teens still spend more time with real friends than with AI companions. Younger teens (13-14) also trust AI advice more than 15-17-year-olds do.

Common Sense Media does not consider social AI companions suitable for under-18s and recommends that this age group not use such apps. The organization has no enforcement power, so this is a recommendation. According to the organization's [parents' guide](https://www.commonsensemedia.org/articles/parents-ultimate-guide-to-ai-companions-and-relationships), these apps can claim to be real and can lead to emotional attachment, and the risk is higher for teens experiencing depression, anxiety or loneliness.

Another warning concerns mental health. One study found that chatbot use was associated with worse mental health symptoms. This is a correlation; it has not been proven that the bots cause these symptoms. Still, the view is expressed that relieving emotional distress with a bot could prevent teens from developing the ability to cope with everyday stress. If you notice marked sadness, anxiety or withdrawal in your child, consulting a specialist is the best course.

## Family rules to discuss

Rules written together are more lasting than bans. The following headings can be a starting point:

- **Try it yourself first.** Common Sense Media recommends that parents also try the tools their children use and learn how they work.
- **Open access gradually, based on age.** For young children, use on a shared device and within your sight; as they grow, a family agreement and more independent use within set limits is a reasonable path. This is not an official standard, but a practical suggestion.
- **Clarify the homework rule.** Decide which assignments allow AI, how sources will be credited, and check with the teacher.
- **Have answers verified.** On important topics such as health and relationships, what a bot says is not enough on its own. Common Sense Media warns that teens seeking health advice from AI could face serious harm.
- **Don't share personal information.** To avoid entering details such as name, address, school and photos into chatbots, see our [guide to protecting personal data when using AI](/rehber/yapay-zeka-kullanirken-kisisel-verileri-koruma/).
- **Name a trusted adult for hard moments.** Agree that when they feel sad or lonely, they will first go to a trusted adult. Both the NPR-reported study and Common Sense Media emphasize this point.
- **Talk regularly.** These rules are not something to discuss once and forget; update them as your child grows and as the tools change.

## Finally

AI can help your child learn, but it should not do their thinking for them or replace a friend or an adult. Parental controls are a useful starting point, but they have limits: they do not show chats, they can be bypassed with separate accounts, and they do not on their own provide safety. The strongest protection is a relationship in which your child can openly tell you what they are curious about or what is bothering them.
