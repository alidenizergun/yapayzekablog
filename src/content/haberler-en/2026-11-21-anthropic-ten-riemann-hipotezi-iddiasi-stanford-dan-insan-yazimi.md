Anthropic mathematician Levent Alpöge announced on Friday, November 20, that he had found a proof of the Riemann hypothesis together with Claude. The hypothesis has been open since 1859 and is one of the seven "Millennium Problems" from the Clay Mathematics Institute, each carrying a $1 million prize. According to Decoder, Alpöge's method covers not only the hypothesis but also its broader form, the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis (GRH).

Alpöge published the proof in Lean, a programming language whose results can be checked by computer. According to Decoder, a printed version of the proof would exceed 250,000 pages, so it was shared only in Lean format, in 14 data packages. According to TechCrunch, several mathematicians, including MIT's Andrew Sutherland, confirmed in a Zulip chat that the code compiled successfully. Decoder reports that even if Lean's verification passes, an error could remain if the initial question was translated incorrectly into code, and that checking this should not take experts long. Alpöge, however, plans to write a human-readable version in the coming months.

A few hours later, Stanford professor and American Mathematical Society (AMS) president Ravi Vakil shared on his blog a draft GRH proof written by Alessandro Masullo, a postdoctoral researcher at Stanford. Vakil said the draft is entirely human work and that no AI was used. Masullo says it is the product of 12 years of work. The draft is reported to have been published earlier than planned because of Anthropic's announcement. According to Decoder, Brian Conrad had been reviewing the draft for months.

## Verification not yet complete

Because Masullo's draft has not been translated into Lean, it is hard to say anything about its correctness for now. About eight hours later, Vakil updated his post to say that, with the help of different AI models, some people had reported problems in the draft. He wrote that these problems need to be resolved in a way that satisfies the community, and that he makes no claim about the strategy's correctness. As for Alpöge's proof, TechCrunch writes that most of the mathematics community appears confident the Lean result will hold, but that it will take time for humans to understand it.

## The Navier-Stokes claim

According to Scientific American, Alpöge and Tristan Buckmaster of New York University shared a new AI-assisted proof for the Navier-Stokes equations on the same day. OpenAI's solution, presented in September, was criticized by experts as exploiting a loophole because it relied on an artificial, fine-tuned forcing assumption. The pair say they have closed that gap. Buckmaster said on Mastodon that the text he shared is unpolished and that the certificate for the result will be published soon.

Alpöge says the announcement's coinciding with the week of Anthropic's IPO was bad timing, and stresses that it was not a promotional move.
